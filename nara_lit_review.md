# Literature review: structured-output compliance, LoRA, distillation, constrained decoding, and RLVR

*Grounding for the findings note "Structured-output compliance in a LoRA-tuned vision-language model for archival temporal reasoning."*

This review answers the seven open questions raised in the findings note, anchoring each to retrieved primary literature. The short version: the note's central move — separating cosmetic serialization from reasoning content and re-scoring — is not an idiosyncratic reading of one experiment. It is the same correction the recent structured-output literature has converged on, and several of the note's observations (the prompt-vs-decoder locus of format cost, the failure-mode "whack-a-mole" of prompt iteration, the reward-hacking risk of an un-normalized verifier) have named analogues and direct precedents. Where the note's situation is genuinely under-studied — closed-vocabulary retention under conservative LoRA specifically, and validating an aggregate accuracy number against a procedurally-derived proxy label in archival ML — the review says so and points to the nearest transferable methodology.

The two papers the note already cites both verify cleanly. **Yue et al. 2025** is [*Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) (Yang Yue, Zhiqi Chen, Rui Lu et al.; NeurIPS 2025 Oral), which argues RLVR sharpens and re-weights reasoning paths already latent in the base model rather than expanding the capability frontier. **Shao et al. 2025** is [*Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947) (Rulin Shao, Shuyue Stella Li, Rui Xin et al.), which shows RLVR with GRPO can produce large benchmark gains from rewards that are random or even negatively correlated with correctness. Both descriptions in the note match the papers.

## Q1 — Format-compliance vs reasoning-capability confound in structured-output evaluation

This is the best-supported of the seven questions, and the note's framing is squarely in line with a fast-moving 2024–2026 literature. The confound the note describes — a headline "8% valid" number that reads as a reasoning failure but is almost entirely a serialization failure — is now a recognized problem, and the field has moved from *observing* format-induced degradation to *decomposing* it.

The observation that requiring structured output degrades measured task performance was crystallized by [*Let Me Speak Freely?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442) (Tam et al. 2024), which reported a significant decline in reasoning under format restrictions, worsening with stricter constraints. That paper entangled two things the note is careful to separate, and the more recent work pulls them apart. [*The Format Tax*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03616) (2026) makes the decisive diagnostic point: most of the degradation "enters at the prompt" — the format-*requesting instruction* itself costs accuracy, before any decoder constraint is applied — and prescribes exactly the remedy the note stumbled onto empirically, namely decoupling reasoning from formatting by generating freeform first and reformatting in a second pass. [*Capacity, Not Format*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09410) (2026) refines this further with information-matched prose controls and a schema-complexity gradient, showing the cost is capacity-dependent: models with headroom absorb JSON constraints without degradation, while models near their limits degrade through truncation and other mechanisms. This is directly relevant to an 8B student — a small model is more likely to be in the capacity-constrained regime where the serialization overhead bites.

On the specific question of established practice for separating the two axes: yes, this now exists, though it is recent rather than canonical. [*Quantifying the Impact of Structured Output Format*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21791) (2025) applies causal inference to disentangle format's effect on generation quality from confounds, explicitly criticizing prior "one-way findings" from weakly-controlled comparisons. On the evaluation-design side, [*The Structured Output Benchmark*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25359) (2026) argues that benchmarks must score *value correctness* separately from *schema compliance* — precisely the distinction the note draws when it observes that the reasoning content was correct while only the string serialization failed. There is not, as far as the retrieved literature shows, a single crisp named phenomenon (no "serialization confound" term of art has stuck), but the *practice* — decouple content from format, control for it in evaluation, and don't read schema-invalidity as a reasoning verdict — is established and converging.

**Bottom line for the project:** the note's reframing is the mainstream corrective, not a local rationalization. The strongest citation to lean on is *The Format Tax* for the prompt-locus finding and *Capacity, Not Format* for why an 8B model specifically is vulnerable; *The Structured Output Benchmark* justifies the evaluation split methodologically.

## Q2 — LoRA / low-rank fine-tuning and closed-vocabulary retention

Here the literature supports the *general* mechanism the note hypothesizes but does not (yet) contain a study of the *specific* claim. The specific claim is: conservative LoRA (rank 8, low LR, 2 epochs) under-fits a narrow closed-vocabulary constraint relative to full fine-tuning, as a direct consequence of the same conservatism chosen to avoid catastrophic forgetting.

The general mechanism is well established. [*LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673) (Biderman et al. 2024) is the load-bearing reference: in standard low-rank settings LoRA substantially underperforms full fine-tuning at *acquiring* a target domain, while better preserving base-model capability outside that domain — and the paper frames this explicitly as a trade-off, with LoRA acting as a stronger regularizer than weight decay or dropout. That is exactly the two-sided coin the note describes: the same low-rank conservatism that protects general capability also limits how sharply the adapter can overwrite the pretrained prior — and reconstructing a closed vocabulary against the model's prior for natural-English synonyms ("medium"/"very_high" instead of the legal token) is precisely a case where you *want* to overwrite the prior. [*A Comparative Analysis of LLM Adaptation: SFT, LoRA, and ICL*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00130) (2025) reinforces the framing in data-scarce regimes, positioning full fine-tuning's catastrophic-forgetting cost against LoRA's weaker skill acquisition.

What the retrieved literature does **not** contain is a controlled study isolating *closed-vocabulary / enum-token compliance* as the dependent variable across LoRA rank and learning-rate settings. The claim that conservative LoRA specifically under-fits enumerable-vocabulary constraints is a reasonable extrapolation from "LoRA learns less," but it is an extrapolation. The note's own ablation is arguably novel evidence here: it demonstrates that the *checkpoint already contained* the correct dating judgments and that the residual failures were serialization, which is a slightly different claim than "LoRA failed to learn the vocabulary" — it suggests the vocabulary was learnable but was never *specified*, which is Q3's territory. This matters for how the finding should be written up: the cleaner causal story is the teacher/student prompt asymmetry (Q3), with "conservative LoRA under-fits narrow constraints" as a contributing hypothesis that the current experiment does not cleanly isolate from the prompt-specification effect. A rank/LR sweep holding the prompt fixed would be the experiment that turns the hypothesis into evidence.

**Bottom line:** cite *LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less* for the capability/acquisition trade-off; be explicit that the closed-vocabulary-specific version of the claim is not yet directly established and is partially confounded with the prompt-specification gap in the current setup.

## Q3 — In-context schema specification vs weight-encoded compliance in distillation

The note's most interesting mechanistic claim — that the student failed because it was never *given* the vocabulary and grammar the teacher had in-context, only clean demonstrations of them — maps onto a real and somewhat under-appreciated theme, though there is no single paper named after this exact asymmetry.

The closest direct evidence is [*Exploring Format Consistency for Instruction Tuning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15504) (Liang et al. 2023), which shows that inconsistency in instruction *format* across training data harms generalization, and that unifying the format (their UIT framework) improves it. The transferable lesson is that models are sensitive to whether the format/schema is stated consistently and explicitly, not merely demonstrated — which supports the note's remedy of giving the student the same explicit rubric the teacher had. On the distillation side, [*Knowledge Distillation Using Frontier Open-source LLMs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18588) (Nayak et al. 2024) studies exactly the pipeline shape in the note — a large teacher (405B) generating synthetic training data for 8B/70B students — and examines the role of synthetic-data properties in what transfers, though it does not isolate in-context-schema transfer specifically.

The note has identified a **plausible, under-documented failure mode of synthetic-data distillation pipelines** rather than a named, well-studied one. The general principle that "clean demonstrations of a constraint" and "an explicit statement of the constraint" are different training signals is widely assumed but rarely ablated head-to-head in the distillation setting. The note's three-variant ablation (vague field names → enum enumeration → full rubric) is, in effect, a small controlled study of this exact question, and it is more direct evidence for the claim than anything retrieved. This is a place where the project's own result is a contribution, not just a confirmation — worth framing that way, and worth a targeted follow-up (does copying the teacher's system prompt verbatim into the student's SFT prompt close the gap at training time, not just inference time?).

**Bottom line:** the asymmetry is real and lightly documented; cite *Format Consistency for Instruction Tuning* for the format-explicitness principle and the frontier-distillation paper for the pipeline shape, but present the note's own ablation as the primary evidence and flag the head-to-head "schema-given vs schema-demonstrated" distillation experiment as an open, publishable question.

## Q4 — Grammar-constrained / guided decoding vs prompt engineering for schema compliance

This is a mature area, and the literature strongly supports the note's implicit conclusion: given that prompt iteration plateaued at 50% raw compliance, constrained decoding is the correct next tool for the *enumerable* parts of the schema (the closed vocabularies and the JSON structure), with a caveat about the custom EDTF grammar.

The foundational methods are [*Efficient Guided Generation for Large Language Models*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09702) (Willard & Louf 2023, the Outlines approach), which reformulates constrained generation as finite-state-machine transitions and builds a vocabulary index to enforce regular expressions and context-free grammars, and [*Grammar-Constrained Decoding for Structured NLP Tasks without Finetuning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13971) (Geng et al. 2023), which shows arbitrary formal grammars can constrain generation to *guarantee* structural validity with no fine-tuning at all. Production engines have since made this efficient: [*XGrammar*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15100) (Dong et al. 2024) is the CFG-constrained engine now underlying much of vLLM/SGLang guided decoding, and [*SynCode*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.01632) (Ugare et al. 2024) targets JSON/YAML/code grammars specifically, driving syntax errors toward zero. [*Flexible and Efficient Grammar-Constrained Decoding*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05111) (2025) addresses the subword-tokenizer-vs-grammar alignment problem that is the real engineering obstacle to enforcing a *custom* grammar like EDTF.

The critical nuance — and the reason constrained decoding is not a free lunch for this project — comes from [*JSONSchemaBench*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10868) (Geng et al. 2025), the first systematic evaluation of constrained decoding across frameworks. Its findings map directly onto the note's residual EDTF problems: constraint frameworks reliably enforce JSON *structure* and closed enums, but coverage and quality vary, and enforcing structure does not guarantee the *content* placed into that structure is correct. Constrained decoding would have eliminated the enum-drift errors ("medium" → forced into {"high","med","low"}) with certainty rather than the note's 50% plateau, and a well-specified CFG could enforce EDTF *interval syntax*. What it cannot do is decide *whether* a date should be a point or an interval — that is a content judgment, and forcing the grammar can even induce the "wrap every date in a degenerate interval" pathology the note observed if the grammar makes intervals the path of least resistance. So the comparative evidence says: constrained decoding dominates prompting for the closed-vocabulary and JSON-structure layer (guaranteed vs 50% plateau), is *applicable* to EDTF but requires care in grammar design, and is orthogonal to the content-correctness questions the note showed were already being answered correctly.

**Bottom line:** for an 8B model the evidence favors constrained/grammar-guided decoding over further prompt iteration for the enumerable schema layer — cite JSONSchemaBench for the systematic comparison and Outlines/XGrammar/SynCode as the method stack. Design the EDTF grammar to make point-dates and intervals equally reachable so the decoder doesn't manufacture the interval-overuse failure.

## Q5 — Prompt-iteration failure-mode shifting

The note's sharpest qualitative observation — that each prompt fix traded one mechanical failure mode for a different, previously-absent one rather than monotonically improving — has strong support as a *symptom* of prompt brittleness, though it is characterized more as high-variance sensitivity than as a named "failure-mode shifting" law.

The canonical result is [*Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324) (Sclar et al. 2023, the FormatSpread work): meaning-preserving changes to prompt formatting produce a large *spread* in performance, establishing that model behavior under prompting is unstable with respect to non-semantic choices. Subsequent work quantifies this systematically — [*ProSA*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12405) (Zhuo et al. 2024) shows sensitivity varies at the instance level and relates to model confidence; [*Benchmarking Prompt Sensitivity*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06065) (2025) frames predicting which prompts will fail as its own task; and [*Towards LLMs Robustness to Changes in Prompt Format Styles*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06969) (2025) names the phenomenon "prompt brittleness" and argues for robustness methods over the traditional search for a single optimal prompt.

The gap between this literature and the note's exact observation is worth stating precisely. The literature robustly establishes that prompts are brittle and that outcomes swing non-monotonically with prompt changes — which *implies* that a fix targeting one error can perturb the model into a different error region. But a specifically-characterized "you fix failure A and failure B appears" dynamic, with guidance on *when to stop iterating*, is not something the retrieved papers name or model directly. The practical implication is nonetheless well-supported and is essentially the note's own conclusion: because prompt-space optimization is a high-variance search with no monotonicity guarantee, the robustness literature's prescription is to stop treating prompt search as convergent and instead constrain the output (Q4) or change the weights (Q2/Q3). The note's plateau-at-50%-with-shifting-errors is a textbook instance of why "prompt until it works" has no well-defined stopping point.

**Bottom line:** cite FormatSpread as the foundational spurious-sensitivity result and the prompt-brittleness/robustness papers for the modern framing; note honestly that "failure-mode shifting" is a reasonable description of an under-formalized dynamic, and that the literature's actionable guidance is to switch from prompt search to constrained decoding or fine-tuning rather than to keep iterating — which is what the note did.

## Q6 — Reward robustness to format noise in RLVR / GRPO for structured outputs

This question is directly load-bearing for the project's planned next phase, and the literature strongly validates the note's specific worry: an RLVR/GRPO reward function that parses this JSON schema *without* an equivalent repair/normalization step would score serialization noise instead of reasoning quality, and that is a concrete instance of a documented reward-hacking risk.

GRPO itself comes from [*DeepSeekMath*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300) (Shao et al. 2024), which introduced group-relative policy optimization — estimating advantages from the relative scores of a *group* of sampled outputs rather than a learned value model. The mechanism the note is right to worry about follows from this design: GRPO's signal is the *spread of rewards within a group*, so if a chunk of that spread is driven by cosmetic format noise (one sample happens to emit valid EDTF, another emits a truncated interval, with identical underlying dating), the group-relative advantage is partly computed over serialization luck rather than reasoning — noise directly in the quantity GRPO optimizes.

The reward-hacking-on-format precedent is explicit. [*ODIN*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07319) (Chen et al. 2024) studies reward hacking on response *length/format* — well-formatted, verbose outputs earning high reward disconnected from quality — and its remedy is a disentangled reward plus a more reliable evaluation protocol, which is the archetype for stripping a format signal out of the reward path. Even more on-point, [*Reward Hacking Mitigation using Verifiable Composite Rewards*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15557) (2025) documents RLVR models exploiting *non-standard reasoning formats* to game the reward and proposes composite verifiable rewards as the fix — the closest published analogue to the note's proposed repair/normalization layer inside the reward function. The two papers the note already cites bound the stakes: [Shao et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947) shows RLVR can post large gains from rewards *uncorrelated* with correctness (so a reward contaminated by format noise can absolutely "work" on the metric while teaching the wrong thing), and [Yue et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837) shows RLVR mostly re-weights existing base-model behavior — meaning if the base checkpoint's dating is already correct (as the note establishes), the *only* thing left for a format-noisy reward to sharpen is the serialization, which is the reward-hacking failure in its purest form.

What the retrieved literature does **not** provide is a turnkey best-practice recipe for "how much unrepaired format noise degrades GRPO's advantage signal" as a quantified curve, or a standardized repair-layer design for grammar-constrained reward parsing. That specific quantification appears to be open. But the qualitative guidance is unambiguous and directly supported: normalize/repair the output to canonical form *before* the reward parses it (so the reward scores content, not serialization), or separate a small format-validity reward term from the reasoning-outcome reward (the ODIN/composite-reward pattern) so the two signals don't contaminate each other. A cleaner alternative the note is well-positioned to use: constrain decoding during rollouts (Q4) so every sampled output is schema-valid by construction, removing format variance from the group *before* the reward ever sees it.

**Bottom line:** the concern is real and precedented — cite DeepSeekMath for GRPO, ODIN and the verifiable-composite-rewards paper for format/reasoning reward-hacking and its mitigations, and the project's existing Yue/Shao citations to bound why a format-contaminated reward is especially dangerous here. Put a normalization/repair layer in the reward path (or constrain rollouts) and consider a factored reward. The exact degradation-vs-noise quantification is an open question worth measuring.

## Q7 — Evaluating accuracy against heuristic / proxy labels (weak supervision, archival ML)

This question — how to separate genuine model error from correct disagreement with a noisy, procedurally-derived proxy label, especially when the model's *job* is to detect divergence from that heuristic — has a strong, mature answer in the weak-supervision literature and a specific, well-established methodological prescription. The archival-NLP-specific precedent is thinner but real.

The foundational framework is [*Data Programming*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07723) (Ratner et al. 2016) and its system realization [*Snorkel*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10160) (Ratner et al. 2017): the entire premise is that "ground truth" is often a set of heuristics/labeling functions with *unknown accuracies and correlations*, and the machinery exists to model and *denoise* those sources without access to true labels. This is exactly the note's situation — the order-type label is a procedurally-derived heuristic — and the weak-supervision framing reconceives such a label not as ground truth to score against but as a noisy source to be modeled. [*AutoWS*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03297) (2023) extends the automated generation of such labeling functions. Crucially for the note's methodological question, the standard practice in this literature is explicit and answers the question directly: **you validate against a small hand-labeled gold/development set before trusting any aggregate number computed against the weak labels.** The weak/heuristic labels train and are scored provisionally; a held-out expert-annotated subset is what calibrates and validates. [*Confident Learning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00068) (Northcutt et al. 2019, the cleanlab method) supplies the complementary tool: it estimates and ranks *label errors in a dataset* from model-vs-label disagreement, which is precisely the instrument for triaging "is this a model error or a bad proxy label?" — disagreements the model is confident about are candidate proxy-label errors, exactly the divergences the note's task is designed to surface.

So the answer to the note's question is: yes, there is clear precedent, and the prescribed methodology is (1) treat the procedural order-type label as a weak/noisy source, not ground truth; (2) build a small hand-checked gold subset and report the aggregate accuracy *against that*, using the weak-label agreement only as a secondary, clearly-labeled signal; and (3) use confident-learning-style disagreement analysis to separate systematic proxy-label error from genuine model error — with the added twist, unique to this task, that *systematic confident disagreement is the intended output*, so it should be inspected qualitatively rather than penalized. Reporting a single accuracy-against-proxy number without the gold subset would, by this literature's standards, conflate the two error types the task is specifically built to distinguish.

For the archival/historical-NLP angle specifically, the retrieved evidence is narrower but confirms the conditions rather than the exact methodology. The [*NER on Historical Documents* survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11406) (Ehrmann et al. 2021) documents that annotation scarcity and noisy inputs are the defining constraints of archival NLP, and [*Investigating OCR-Sensitive Neurons*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16934) (2024) shows label/input noise in historical documents is *systematic* (OCR-correlated), not random — which strengthens the case that an aggregate accuracy number against a proxy label in this domain is untrustworthy without a clean gold anchor. What the search did not surface is a paper doing exactly the note's move — validating a divergence-detector against a small gold subset in an archival setting — so the transfer is from the general weak-supervision methodology into archival ML, which is sound but is the reviewer's synthesis rather than a single citable precedent.

**Bottom line:** cite Data Programming and Snorkel for reframing the proxy label as a noisy source, Confident Learning for the disagreement-triage instrument, and the archival-NLP survey plus OCR-neurons paper for the domain-specific noise conditions. The methodological prescription — hand-checked gold subset before trusting an aggregate, and qualitative inspection of confident disagreements because they are the intended signal — is well-founded; its exact application to archival divergence-detection is a defensible extension rather than a documented recipe.

## Synthesis: where the project stands relative to the literature

Read together, the seven answers point one direction. The note's core reframing (Q1) is the field's current consensus, not a local rationalization; the mechanism it proposes for *why* the student failed (Q2/Q3) is partly established (LoRA learns less) and partly the note's own contribution (schema-given vs schema-demonstrated distillation, which the ablation nearly proves and which is worth writing up as such); and the two forward-looking risks it flags — that prompt iteration won't converge (Q5) and that an un-normalized GRPO reward will hack format (Q6) — are both well-precedented, with concrete mitigations (constrained decoding, factored/repaired rewards) that the literature endorses. The evaluation-methodology question (Q7) has the clearest textbook answer of the seven: validate against a hand-checked gold subset before trusting any accuracy-against-proxy number, and treat confident model–proxy disagreement as signal to inspect rather than error to penalize.

The three genuinely open, potentially-publishable gaps: (a) a closed-vocabulary-compliance-vs-LoRA-rank/LR sweep holding the prompt fixed (Q2); (b) a head-to-head "student given the teacher's in-context schema vs given only clean demonstrations" distillation experiment (Q3); and (c) a quantification of how much unrepaired format noise degrades GRPO's group-relative advantage (Q6).
