**DRAFT (August 2026\)**

“Stream of consciousness” (SoC) narration is a ubiquitous term in modernist studies and fiction generally, yet it has been notoriously difficult to characterize or categorize. As a broad literary genre, SoC has come to delineate works that “attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual” by showing “not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced” (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SoC’s particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. In their large-scale computational study of SoC narration in fiction, Hoyt Long and Richard Jean So characterize the literary technique as inherently ambiguous: “no one can agree on what \[SoC\] is, yet everyone seems to know it when they see it” (346). For such a widely-discussed literary phenomenon, it is striking that it continues to be undecidable in its particularities, and also that it endures as something which wants to be particularized.

The long-standing critical desire to define the mechanisms and features of SoC suggests that it is exactly its porous boundaries which make it so available for analysis. Decades of narratological scholarship, from Robert Humphrey’s foundational 1954 taxonomy onward, have proposed competing schemes for classifying the way SoC appears in text. But thus far there is no critical consensus on what SoC really is: whether it is solely a literary genre defined by thematic concerns unreducible to standardized technical schema, or if there are identifiable narrative techniques consistently used by one author or many, and if so, what those techniques are (some have proposed interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and so forth) and their distribution. For, while almost everyone agrees that SoC *is* a genre more than anything, a work of fiction which aims to represent the inner workings of the mind, almost everyone also attempts at one point or another to describe how such a project works technically on the level of syntax, language, and device. SoC is not simply subject matter, it is a mode of writing developed to uniquely fit its subject matter.

We argue that this persistent scholarly disagreement is not a defect to be engineered away, but actually the most useful signal a computational method can capture about the form. We want to test the current understanding of SoC against the very texts which informed that understanding, to find where our scholarly theories both hold up and start to get blurry. If we had to explain to ourselves, concretely, what categories and features of SoC there are, and where they shade into each other, could we? What happens to our understanding of SoC within a text when we attempt to categorize pieces of that text with these distinctions? Our chapter makes two arguments which build on each other. First, we take seriously the injunction to apply a comprehensive typology of SoC to a whole text, thinking through what it means to try to account for an entire literary genre through technique. We use Humphrey’s taxonomy for this purpose, not because it is definitive but simply because we find it necessary to have a taxonomy in the first place. We show that, when applied to an entire work, this technical approach falls apart; five different LLM models, working from Humphrey’s typology, consistently disagree on how they “interpret” SoC passages. Second, in the face of the dissolution of our ability to schematically apply a SoC typology based on technique, we suggest we should shift our questions about SoC: away from identifying patterns and toward ambiguity and middle space, the places in SoC novels where techniques and forms blur and bleed into each other. \[add more on methodology of this\]. These arguments rely, also, on a thoughtful application of LLM-based methodology. LLMs allow us to at once approach a structural problem through computation but also to abandon that structural angle once we expose its flaws. When analyzing a literary form that has no set categories, types, or techniques, we need a computational method that can account for that indeterminacy, and reasoning models allow us to consistently apply and evaluate complex, interpretive criteria.

Our corpus centers on Virginia Woolf’s *Mrs Dalloway* and *To the Lighthouse*, alongside a comparative set spanning Dorothy Richardson, James Joyce, William Faulkner, and May Sinclair. Woolf presents a useful test case for our questions because of her own “ambiguous” relationship to SoC, as both a genre and a set of techniques. Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of SoC narration, but, as Dora Zhang has counterintuitively noted, “despite the ubiquitous familiarity of \[SoC\] in general, and its association with Woolf in particular, her innovations in this arena have not been clearly recognized” (133). Woolf is thus highly legible for understanding the categories of SoC generally, and she also is perhaps in need of further analysis which acknowledges the complex undecidability of her SoC technique. Indeed, crafting classificatory and interpretive ambiguity is one of Woolf’s primary aims, to create fiction which exposes “the limits of what we can know of other people” as well as the way “we read each other according to such predetermined categories” (Zhang 141). This for Woolf is always a writerly problem as much as it is a social or representational one, and her SoC intricately encodes the “limits” of determinacy in its very movements between narrative modes.

**Woolf’s Stream of Consciousness as Genre or Technique**

It is widely agreed that SoC is not a single narrative technique, but an entire literary genre and subject matter itself, an overarching project of how to represent an individual consciousness. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in their introduction to a special issue on SoC, “a definitive definition of the elusive label ‘stream of consciousness’ is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized” (2). This often manifests when we attempt to account for SoC as a total category, rather than focus specifically on particular authors’ modes of representing the “stream” of experience and the mind. Bowler and Drewery, citing Humphrey, emphasize the irreducibility of the SoC genre across authors and texts, that it cannot be boiled down to one thing: “all stream of consciousness writing is formally specific only to itself, but there has been a tendency to classify all such writing as adhering to the same technique or single form” (3). Despite this understanding, SoC undeniably comes with a set of unique formal and syntactic characteristics that differentiate it from other narrative modes. Jan Mieszkowski describes SoC primarily through its sentential innovations: as a syntactic structure in addition to a theme. He writes that, in comparison to more traditional forms of representing consciousness like interior monologue, SoC renders interiority “verbally as strings of words that may or may not respect conventional grammatical, logical, or rhetorical paradigms … leveling or at least reconfiguring the traditional hierarchies ordering the parts of a sentence” (169-70). The linguistic, structural, formal understanding of SoC means that it is an identifiable set of techniques. Franco Moretti even argues that the “intensity” of SoC in some texts “can actually be ‘measured’” according to the frequency of each passage’s “formal characteristics, parataxis, random associations, incorrect grammar, extension of the present moment,” which “fluctuate continually” over the course of various passages (171). Many others, even while taking into account the complexity and idiosyncrasy of SoC, make similar claims toward a technical understanding of the literary genre. SoC is always made up of formal choices as well as thematic ones.

Woolf’s position in this conversation is interesting because she is both highly recognized as an innovator of SoC technique, and yet her particular contribution to the form is undertheorized. This may be in part because the inherent capaciousness itself of SoC has made it difficult to identify and classify concretely and systematically; there are a number of assumptions about SoC’s form that still persist despite the push toward recognizing its variety. We argue that three particular assumptions about SoC’s techniques are part of the discrepancy between Woolf’s long-standing association with the rise of SoC and our particularized understanding of her version of it. First, Woolf’s relative adherence to grammatical sentence structure makes her writing seem more constructed rather than organic, which resists conceptions of SoC as something that renders thought “naturally” on the page; second, Woolf as a SoC innovator has historically come second to Joyce, which has perpetuated the narrative that she is slightly less innovative when compared to the standards set by Joyce’s own SoC techniques; and third, that her predominant use of free indirect discourse or, to use Humphrey’s term, “indirect interior monologue,” leads to the assumption that her narrative technique is more conventional. These assumptions, as they appear in scholarship, are interrelated; they all boil down to the idea that there is a standard, and more innovative, narrative about the development of SoC as a technique that Woolf does not quite adhere to.

Originating from William James’ description of human thought as “flow” rather than “jointed”-ness, the metaphor of the “stream” of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. Novelist May Sinclair first imported the term into literature when reviewing the first three novel-chapters of Dorothy Richardson’s *Pilgrimage*, and with it the term’s metaphorical associations of flow, “moments of … consciousness pass\[ing\] one by one” (58). Though scholars and practitioners of SoC themselves have complicated and taken issue with this understanding of SoC, its origins in psychology have predetermined its association with both unformedness and single-mindedness. Such an understanding puts Woolf in the back seat, for “while \[SoC\] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom’s soliloquy at the end of *Ulysses*, Woolf’s innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly” (Zhang 133). Woolf’s SoC innovations may be “quieter” than others’ because her writing has been characterized as more grammatical and constructed than loose and organic. Moretti describes, for instance, Woolf’s narratorial control in *To the Lighthouse*: “If, at certain moments, Lily Briscoe is seized by panic and on the point of losing control, Woolf’s syntax is able to check any such drift, and give form to chaos … Nothing ungrammatical, here; no levelling parataxis, or random associations. In sentences woven into a lucid sequence leading towards their goal – Proust after the stream of consciousness, as it were – narrative consciousness has regained the upper hand” (172). The standard against which Woolf’s syntax is evaluated is “chaos;” Moretti’s description of Woolf is written in negations, as he notes an expected style which is *absent* in the SoC passage.

Most of all, Woolf is compared to Joyce on these grounds of irregularity, unformedness, and chaos. Such characterizations, while perhaps accurate in their descriptions, suggest a hierarchy of experimentalism in which Joyce’s narration is seen as more radical than everyone else’s. An early monograph on SoC by Shiv K. Kumar proposes Joyce’s writing to be the “perfection of the stream of consciousness” (qtd. Bowler and Drewery 4). In a reading of one passage from \[Ulysses?\], Anne Fernihough writes that “Joyce’s technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf’s, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the *chaos* of the inner life, and the raw, unfinished quality of our thoughts and impressions, as well as the sheer speed with which they meld into one another” (Fernihough 66, emphasis in original). David Dowling notes Woolf’s relative “control” over even the passages which are the most wandering: “Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a ‘tidy mind’ compared with that of Joyce” (48). These comparisons emphasize the idea that SoC should in its most technically pure form prioritize the organic “stream” quality embedded in James’ metaphor, what Fernihough describes as “an undiscriminating and unstoppable consciousness in which there is no editing out and no hierarchy of thought” (67). And though many critics have noted that this metaphor has been a contentious one since the beginning (Richardson herself took issue with it), that has not come hand-in-hand with decoupling Joyce’s primary association with the form. That association still results in Joyce overshadowing other SoC innovators, Woolf especially.

The sense that Joyce was more experimental than Woolf comes, perhaps, from each of their relationships to traditional narrative modes. Despite the fact that Joyce also employs a plethora of narrative techniques, Woolf’s particular SoC mode has been seen as more in line with free indirect discourse, a very canonical technique and thus less clearly new. In her study of the representation of consciousness in fiction, *Transparent Minds*, Dorrit Cohn highly associates Woolf with the technique she calls “narrated monologue,” defined as “a character’s mental discourse in the guise of the narrator’s discourse” and a very close analogue to free indirect discourse (14). This technique, she writes, “needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction” (115). In other words, Woolf’s primary choice of SoC technique was less of a clean “break” from tradition and more of a subtle shift, still reflected in style but less so than the “rival technique of the unsignaled ‘Joycean’ monologue” (112). Dora Zhang’s chapter convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionizes free indirect discourse more than others have acknowledged previously, but it is perhaps Woolf’s continued association with the technique that puts closer analysis of her SoC on pause. Free indirect discourse is widely studied and attributed to many authors, which may make it seem less imperative to continually investigate Woolf’s version of it.

Taken together, these three assumptions describe a Woolf who is legible to SoC criticism only by comparison, and only ever in the negative: measured against a chaos she declines to produce, a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, and a free indirect discourse so familiar that it no longer seems to require close technical scrutiny. This comparative frame, we suggest, has obscured a more basic question that the field has not yet put to Woolf’s fiction directly, not how her stream of consciousness differs from Joyce’s, but what, technically, is actually happening across the whole of a Woolf novel when consciousness is being rendered: how much of it is narrated monologue, how much omniscient description, how much something else, and how confidently those distinctions can even be drawn from passage to passage. Answering that question requires more than another close reading of a single passage, however skillful; it requires applying a typology of SoC’s techniques across an entire text, consistently, and seeing what holds and what does not. That is the test to which we now turn.

**Testing the Affordances and Problems of Typology with LLMs**

As of now, we have two contrasting problems: that a typology of SoC “technique” is still widely contested, but also that Woolf’s SoC demands a closer technical eye. It is perhaps impossible (and not particularly interesting) to create a totalizing technical schema for the form. Yet some structure is needed to make substantial claims about SoC’s techniques, even if they are extremely various. In order to ask, “What specifically characterizes Woolf’s SoC technique, and how is it distinctive from that of others?” we have to have a sense of the composition and distribution of those techniques in the first place. If we wanted to figure out how to better characterize and classify Woolf’s particular mode of SoC, how could we do so without first having a typology of some sort by which to categorize passages?

The element of computation both adds to and helps approach this dilemma. Computationally measuring something in a text too demands a structure, a methodology by which to systematically identify patterns and divergences. Long and So’s article which computationally models SoC by identifying key structural and linguistic elements suggests that it is possible to think of SoC as a literary mode that does have “some degree of formal unification” (350). They take as their starting point the “persistent claim that SOC arises through a discrete set of these features” to then look for SoC in texts that “fall well outside SOC scholarship”, an attempt to expand the SoC novel-genre by applying its measurable features to a wider swath of texts (347, 354). Our set of questions is similar to Long and So’s, but the key difference is that we are interested in moving *in* rather than expanding out. We wanted to test the affordances of applying a technical typology to classify individual passages within a single text instead of searching for SoC features in other texts. We also both test the boundaries and limits of SoC typology, but with quite different methodologies. Their approach is purely structural and stylometric, in that the features they measure are entirely numerical and produces replicable statistics about the recurrence of particular stylistic elements in text. We take SoC’s interpretive undecidability and difficulty as part of our method itself. If we want to ask, for example, “How much does Woolf actually use a technique like ‘narrated monologue’?,” we need a computational approach that can account for the complexity of that very category. An NLP approach can count things like sentence length, type-token-ratios, and proportion of ellipses (features which Long and So measure, among others), but it cannot decide how much distance there is between the narrator and the character, or if a passage is utilizing a “space-montage” descriptive technique. With learning models, on the other hand, we can loosen and even sometimes abandon the structural angle, allowing us to consistently evaluate passages based on more interpretive and complex criteria.

To begin our attempt to classify SoC techniques in text, we started from Robert Humphrey’s early SoC taxonomy in *Stream of Consciousness in the Modern Novel*. We turned to Humphrey not because we necessarily agree with all his claims (or even his methodology), but simply as a starting point for classification. Humphrey classifies SoC into both broad techniques of presentation, direct interior monologue, indirect interior monologue, description by an omniscient author, and soliloquy, and a further set of devices through which those techniques are realized on the page, among them free association, sensory image and memory, and the montage-like juxtaposition of time or space. In practice, these two levels of Humphrey’s schema blur into each other more than his own chapter divisions imply: a passage of indirect interior monologue is very often also an instance of free association or reverie, and his own readings continually reach for a technique-plus-device pairing to describe a single passage rather than a single clean label.

To make this schema usable at the scale of a whole novel, we operationalized Humphrey’s categories into twelve discrete labels, flattening his techniques and devices into one taxonomy a model could apply consistently, and adding two categories of our own, hybrid, for passages where two types are inseparably fused, and other\_soc, for passages that are clearly interior but resist every named type, to give the taxonomy somewhere to fail without simply mislabeling the passage. We then had five large language models, independent “readers.” We used “reasoning” models so that each SoC type prediction includes the reasoning trace that the model used to connect a given passage with the taxonomy. We applied the taxonomy across a corpus of twenty-one novels by Woolf, Richardson, Joyce, Faulkner, Henry James, and Ford Madox Ford, resampled at temperature so that each reader judged each passage multiple times rather than once. This resampling matters for what follows: it lets us distinguish a model that is genuinely uncertain about a passage, and says so inconsistently across repeated readings, from a model that is simply wrong once. Where earlier scholarship could observe that critics disagree about SoC, we could now observe, and quantify, whether the same reader disagrees with itself.

Our final results showed that a Humphrey-style typology covers 44.3% of text in the corpus. In other words, less than half of the corpus could be typed at all given Humphrey’s categories. And within that percentage, 72.2% of decisions were contested between different LLM models. Direct interior monologue was the most stable type, followed by indirect interior monologue and omniscient description. The other types are highly contested. Clean textbook fits of Humphrey's categories are the minority reading even where they occur, and full cross-model agreement on “yes, this is clean” is rare. This result suggests both that a single SoC typology is insufficient for understanding comprehensively how even a single text utilizes “SoC techniques,” and that even when the typology reliably classifies certain passages as SoC, there is extreme internal interpretive disagreement on *why* the passage has been identified as such. In other words, even when models agreed that a passage was written with a particular “SoC technique,” they applied Humphrey’s criteria differently in the process of determining that. This confirms the critical conception that SoC is an infinitely variable literary form, one that, by definition, depends on blurring boundaries between narrative modes.

It’s worth dwelling on a few examples of this model disagreement to highlight what, exactly, models disagree about, not merely whether a passage counts as SoC, but which of Humphrey’s categories best accounts for a passage all five readers agree is SoC. The clearest way to see this is a single passage on which trained models reach opposite, confidently argued conclusions.

The two models do not simply choose different labels for this passage, one leaning toward quoted monologue, the other toward omniscient description; they read the identical clause, “he had thought to himself, Who is that lovely girl?”, as evidence for opposite conclusions about how mediated the passage is. For one reader, the clause is a framed but still discrete rendering of Richard’s mental event, close enough to the character’s own words that Humphrey’s typology registers real strain in choosing between a quoted-monologue and an omniscient-description reading; for the other, the same clause is a narratorial summary that cleanly fits omniscient description, and its gloss on the alternative, the “consistent past-perfect narration” and “lack of syntactic fusion between narrator and character voice” that “rule out interior monologue types,” treats the first reader’s sense of strain as simply mistaken. Both models are reading the same three words toward opposite ends of the same scale, and both readings, on their own terms, hold up.

This kind of disagreement, moreover, turns out to be close to the rule rather than the exception. Of the 7,739 passages on which our readers stably disagree, 7,680 of them, more than ninety-nine percent, split along this same line: one reader calling a passage a clean fit for its assigned type, another calling the identical passage a case of boundary tension. Models rarely outright confuse, say, free association with omniscient description. What they disagree about, overwhelmingly, is whether a passage that everyone agrees resembles a given Humphrey category resembles it cleanly enough to count, or whether the resemblance is insufficient, which suggests that Humphrey’s categories are not simply too few, or too vague, to sort passages reliably, but that the sorting itself, the judgment of how much resemblance is enough, is where the taxonomy’s stability actually breaks down, passage by passage, reader by reader.

**Humphrey’s Map, Fully Drawn**

If Humphrey’s typology strains against a persistent kind of ambiguity, it is worth asking what kind of typology it turns out to be in practice. Three of Humphrey’s twelve categories (direct interior monologue, indirect interior monologue, and omniscient description) account for roughly eighty-four percent of every type ever predicted, across all readers, runs, and authors. The remaining nine categories, including several Humphrey treats at length as distinct techniques- free association, space-montage, imagery, the simulation of a state of mind prior to verbalization, reverie or fantasy- divide up what is left, each individually a small fraction of the whole. The twelfth, the category meant to capture SoC’s rendering through typography itself- italics, dashes, unconventional spacing- is never assigned at all, not once, across five readers, six authors, and over fifty-six thousand judgments. Humphrey did answer Long and So’s challenge to specify SoC’s mechanisms rather than simply recognize them; his taxonomy is not, in that sense, undertheorized. It is simply lopsided: a map that describes three provinces in detail and leaves nine largely unpopulated.

This finding also reframes a question we’ve been circling. It would be easy to read the disagreement data above as evidence that Woolf’s prose in particular resists Humphrey’s categories, that her famous ambiguity, her tendency to blur the narrator’s voice into her characters’, is what defeats the typology. The data does not support that reading, or at least not on its own. Broken down by author, the rate at which a passage is judged to fit its assigned type cleanly, rather than under boundary tension, is remarkably uniform: 40.4 percent for Joyce, 43.1 for James, 43.7 for Faulkner, 45.1 for Richardson, 46.0 for Woolf, 49.1 for Ford. Woolf sits, if anything, slightly above the middle of this range, not at some outlying extreme of unclassifiability, and Joyce, canonically the more radical, more technically legible SoC writer by the very reception history this chapter has been describing, strains against Humphrey’s categories very slightly more than Woolf does, not less. The typology’s instability, in other words, is less a Woolf problem than something close to a universal feature of applying Humphrey’s categories to real modernist prose.

Woolf’s particular reputation for narratorial quietness, her association with a supposedly more conventional, more grammatically composed rendering of consciousness, cannot be explained by appeal to Humphrey’s typology at all. If her SoC really were more classifiable, more legible to a technical schema, than Joyce’s, that difference should show up here, in the rate at which her passages resolve cleanly into a single named type; it does not, and both writers turn out to be ambiguous by Humphrey’s own lights at almost exactly the same rate. The question this chapter has to ask next, then, is not which of Humphrey’s categories Woolf’s SoC belongs to, or how cleanly it belongs there, but whether “which category” is even the right question to put to a text this variable. That is where we turn from Humphrey’s discrete types to a different, more explicitly ordinal way of measuring the same phenomenon.

**From Types to Distance: Cohn’s Scale** **as a Computational Pivot**

Dorrit Cohn’s *Transparent Minds* does not ask which of several discrete techniques a passage belongs to. It asks a single, continuous question: how much mediation stands between the narrator’s voice and the character’s consciousness, arranged along a scale running from psycho-narration (the narrator’s own account of a character’s inner life, from outside), through narrated monologue (the technique this chapter has already associated with Woolf, in which the character’s thought is rendered in the narrator’s grammar), to quoted monologue (an interior utterance set off, however lightly, as the character’s own words), and finally to autonomous monologue (thought presented with no narratorial frame at all). Humphrey asks a passage to declare itself as one of twelve named things; Cohn’s scale asks only where, along a single dimension, a passage falls, and, crucially, does not require that answer to be discrete. A passage can sit at 1.4 as easily as at 1 or 2\.

We used this scale as the basis for a second pass over the same corpus, with the same readers scoring each passage’s mediation level on a 0–3 scale rather than assigning it a named type. The appeal of doing this is not that an ordinal score is inherently less contestable than a category label, since reasonable readers can and do disagree about how mediated a passage is, just as they disagree about which Humphrey type fits it, but that an ordinal score changes what disagreement means. Two readers assigning different Humphrey types to the same passage are making a claim that can only be right or wrong; two readers assigning mediation levels of 1 and 2 to the same passage, by contrast, are making claims that are already close to each other by construction, and the distance between them becomes a number we can work with rather than a contradiction we have to resolve.

Before trusting this measure, though, we needed to know whether it was tracking something real about the prose, or simply reproducing whatever a language model’s prior notion of “stream of consciousness” already was. So we tested mediation level against a small set of features computed independently of any model’s judgment: the density of first-person pronouns, the ratio of past to non-past tense, the density of dashes and ellipses, and so on. These are features that a simple script can count without reading for meaning at all. If the scale were merely an artifact of model idiosyncrasy, there is no reason these counts should predict it. They do: passages with a higher proportion of first-person language score reliably lower on the mediation scale, closer to the character’s own voice, and passages with a higher proportion of past-tense narration score reliably higher, closer to the narrator’s, both relationships strong and significant across a sampled validation set. One prediction made going in, that heavier fragment and dash-and-ellipsis punctuation would track lower mediation, the visual signature of a mind interrupting itself, turned out to run backward: passages dense with any kind of internal punctuation, dashes and ellipses and semicolons alike, actually skew toward more mediated, more narratorially controlled prose, which is consistent with this project’s own earlier observation that Woolf’s elaborate, subordinated syntax pulls toward omniscient description rather than away from it. That one feature ran the wrong direction does not undercut the ordinal measure as a whole; if anything, a specific, correctable prediction failing for an identifiable reason is more reassuring than a set of results that had simply confirmed every expectation. The measure, in short, answers to something real in the text, not to a model’s untethered impression of what SoC ought to sound like.

This validation does not, however, dissolve the disagreement the Humphrey typology surfaced. When we looked for a relationship between how much readers disagree about a passage’s mediation level and these same surface features, we found none: no correlation with pronoun density, tense, or punctuation at any strength worth reporting. Boundary tension, in other words, is not simply what happens when a passage’s surface features are themselves ambiguous by some cheap lexical measure; wherever it comes from, it cannot be explained away by counting words, which is in some ways a more useful result than a clean correlation would have been. It tells us that the disagreement Humphrey’s typology exposed was never a measurement problem that a better feature set could quietly resolve, but a genuinely interpretive frontier, one the scale lets us locate with far more precision than the typology did without letting us explain it away.

**Close Reading at Scale: “Time Passes”**

The variance-decomposition results below speak to Woolf’s SoC across whole novels; it is worth also asking what the same method can do at the scale of a single, already-famous crux. “Time Passes,” the short middle section of *To the Lighthouse* in which the Ramsays’ house stands empty for ten years and Mrs Ramsay’s death is disposed of in a single bracketed aside, is a canonical site of exactly this kind of narratorial strangeness in Woolf criticism,a section critics have long treated as doing something formally unusual, without always agreeing on what. It is also, in our data, the single site in two full novels’ worth of scored passages where a reader’s antimimetic flag, reserved for a passage that resists every one of Humphrey’s named types for an explicit textual reason, not merely low confidence, actually fires. That made it worth a properly powered second look, rescoring all thirty-three passages of the section at ten runs per reader instead of three, rather than resting an interpretive claim on a single low-probability hit.

“Time Passes” is famous for its strangeness, and the natural assumption going in was that strangeness would register as *less* mediated prose: thought breaking further free of narratorial control, the mind rendered rawer and closer to its own voice, as in Faulkner’s or Joyce’s more radical passages. It turns out to be the opposite. Across the section, mediation scores skew markedly toward the heavily mediated end of the scale compared to the two novels’ baseline, and the great majority of its stable, SoC-positive passages land as omniscient description, psycho-narration, the *most* mediated point on Cohn’s scale, not the least. Whatever is strange about “Time Passes,” then, is not that Woolf’s narrator recedes; it is that the narrator’s ordinary move, interpreting a mind from outside, “she felt,” “it seemed as if,” keeps firing on content that increasingly has no mind, in any ordinary sense, to interpret.

That displacement sits in almost no space at all: two sentences, out of thirty-three passages, in which mental predicates are given not to a character but to something that is not a person. In one, “certain airs, detached from the body of the wind,” are described entering the house “questioning and wondering,” asking, “Were they allies? Were they enemies?” In the other, the room’s own silence is personified the same way, “weaving” the cries of birds and the drone of the fields into itself and “folding them round the house in silence,” the same rendering of thought-like agency in something that has no mind to think it. On these two sentences, and on almost no others in the whole sample, our two most consistent readers, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max, come apart in a way that is not noise. Across ten repeated readings of the first sentence, GPT-5.4 does not scatter across many answers; it lands, almost evenly, on two distinct positions, each independently reproduced: five readings that hold the passage within Humphrey’s taxonomy, under strain, as a simulation of a state of mind, and five that push it out of the taxonomy altogether, arguing that thought-like inquiry without a “plausible single consciousness to own it” cannot belong to any category built on the assumption that rendered consciousness has a human owner. Qwen3-Max, reading the identical sentence, registers almost no strain at all: nine of ten readings call it a clean instance of omniscient description, the personified airs treated as ordinary narratorial imagery rather than as anything the taxonomy cannot hold.

This is, in miniature, close to the theoretical debate Monika Fludernik and, following her, Jan Alber and Brian Richardson have had about “unnatural” narrative technique more broadly: whether devices that seem to exceed realist consciousness-representation are a genuinely distinct category, or whether they are simply conventions not yet naturalized by a given reader’s expectations. Our data does not answer that debate, but it does show it actually happening in a specific, quotable pair of sentences between two readers with different priors about how far a figure of speech can carry an interior state before it stops being a figure and starts being a problem. It also lets us say something more precise than “Woolf’s SoC sometimes exceeds the typology”: across the whole of two novels, this device, thought-like predicates attached to a non-human antecedent, is the one place, nearly the only place, where whether Humphrey’s categories hold at all depends on which reader, human or machine, is doing the holding.

**Woolf’s Variation, Novel to Novel**

If the whole-corpus data resists a Woolf-specific account of the typology’s instability, and the “Time Passes” data locates one very specific device where it genuinely does break down, a third question remains open: how variable is Woolf’s SoC across her own body of work, novel to novel? The reception history this chapter opened with treats Woolf’s SoC as, in effect, a fixed style, quieter than Joyce’s, more grammatically composed, essentially the same technique deployed across a career. Cohn’s scale lets us test that assumption directly by asking not how Woolf compares to other authors on average, but how much a given novel’s mean mediation score differs from the mean of her other novels.

The answer is that Woolf is, by a wide margin, the least stylistically fixed author in our comparative set. Using a variance-components measure of between-title consistency, an intraclass correlation, corrected for the fact that an author with more novels in the sample is otherwise structurally advantaged, Woolf’s ten novels score 0.561, meaning more than half of the total variation in mediation level across her corpus falls between books rather than within them. Richardson, by contrast, scores 0.001, essentially zero, though this needs an immediate caveat: *Pilgrimage*’s volumes are installments of one continuous narrative rather than independent novels, so near-zero between-volume variance is close to a definitional fact about serial fiction, not a finding about Richardson’s fixed style. James scores 0.008 and Faulkner 0.037, both genuinely independent novels, both genuinely far more consistent from book to book than Woolf. Joyce, the author against whom Woolf’s supposed quietness is most often measured, scores 0.358, closer to Woolf than to James or Faulkner, but still substantially below her.

It would be easy to assume this entire result is *The Waves*, Woolf’s most formally extreme novel, doing all the work, but removing it from the calculation drops Woolf’s score only to 0.199, a large drop, yet still higher than Joyce’s full ten-title figure, and, tellingly, still higher than Joyce’s own score after removing *his* single most extreme outlier, *Ulysses*, which falls to 0.117 once removed. Woolf minus her most unusual novel is still more variable, book to book, than Joyce minus his. Nor is the result an artifact of including *Flush* and *Orlando*, Woolf’s two genre-heterogeneous mock-biographies, in a sample otherwise made up of more conventional novels; excluding both actually raises Woolf’s score slightly, to 0.586. However the calculation is sliced, the finding holds: Woolf’s reputation for a settled, recognizable “quiet” technique describes, at most, nine of her ten novels clustered in a comparatively narrow band, and even that band is not uniform, ranging from *Flush* and *Night and Day* at the most heavily mediated end to *Between the Acts* and *The Years* noticeably further along the scale, plus one outright departure in *The Waves*, more radically unmediated than either of Faulkner’s novels. “Quiet” was never a single style. It was nine restrained but not identical deployments of restraint, and one refusal of it.

**Differentiating Woolf on Her Own Terms**

This data does not produce a corrected measurement of how quiet Woolf’s SoC really is relative to Joyce’s, nor a final answer to whether she belongs among literature’s more or less radical stream-of-consciousness writers. We think it points toward a different, and better, question than the comparative one criticism has been asking for a century. Woolf’s SoC does not need to be placed on a scale that runs from Joycean chaos to narratorial control in order to be described with precision; it can be described in its own terms, and those terms turn out to have a very particular shape. Nine novels sit in a restrained, though internally varied, band of comparatively mediated prose, themselves not identical to one another; one novel departs from that band entirely, more unmediated than anything in our comparative corpus. And within an otherwise legible, thoroughly typology-compliant *Lighthouse* and *Dalloway*, the taxonomy’s one real point of failure is not diffuse or general but locatable to the sentence: two moments in “Time Passes” where mental predicates attach themselves to wind and silence rather than to any person, and where whether that counts as stream of consciousness at all depends on a reader’s willingness to naturalize personification as psycho-narration. This is not, finally, a story about distance from Joyce, but about restraint as a repeated and variable choice, about one deliberate departure from that choice, and about a single device, non-human interiority, sitting at the exact edge of what “rendering a mind” can be asked to mean.

This is also, we want to suggest, what a computational method contributes to a problem that close reading alone has spent a century worrying at without resolving. It was never going to settle whether SoC is a genre or a technique, or produce a typology finer than Humphrey’s that finally holds. But it can apply the typology we already have with total consistency across an entire body of work, notice with precision where and how often that consistency breaks down, and follow the break all the way to a sentence, a device, a pair of readers disagreeing for reasons each can actually articulate. Long and So were right that everyone knows SoC when they see it. This chapter’s contribution is a way of finding, systematically, the handful of sentences where even the models trained to know it cannot agree, and of treating those sentences not as noise to be filtered out but as the most precise available description of what Woolf’s stream of consciousness actually is.
