**DRAFT (August 2026\) — alternate version, Cohn-centered**

"Stream of consciousness" (SoC) narration is a ubiquitous term in modernist studies and fiction generally, yet it has been notoriously difficult to characterize or categorize. As a broad literary genre, SoC has come to delineate works that "attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual" by showing "not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced" (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SoC's particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. In their large-scale computational study of SoC narration in fiction, Hoyt Long and Richard Jean So characterize the literary technique as inherently ambiguous: "no one can agree on what \[SoC\] is, yet everyone seems to know it when they see it" (346). For such a widely-discussed literary phenomenon, it is striking that it continues to be undecidable in its particularities, and also that it endures as something which wants to be particularized.

Most attempts to resolve that undecidability have reached for a typology: a set of named techniques- interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and so on- into which a given passage of prose can be sorted. Robert Humphrey's 1954 study is the foundational instance and remains the field's most-cited point of reference, but a typology built from named, discrete categories has a specific cost: it asks a passage to declare itself as one thing among several, when what critics have actually described, for a century, is a matter of degree. Dorrit Cohn's 1978 *Transparent Minds* reframes the question accordingly. Rather than asking which of several techniques a passage belongs to, Cohn asks a single, continuous question: how much mediation stands between the narrator's voice and the character's consciousness, arranged along a scale running from psycho-narration (the narrator's own account of a character's inner life, from outside), through narrated monologue (the character's thought rendered in the narrator's own grammar, without quotation), to quoted monologue (an interior utterance set off, however lightly, as the character's own words), and finally to autonomous monologue (thought presented with no narratorial frame at all). This chapter takes Cohn's scale, not Humphrey's typology, as its starting point and its principal computational instrument, and asks what a large corpus of stream-of-consciousness prose looks like when read along it,  resampled, at scale, by multiple independent readers, human and machine,  with Virginia Woolf's fiction at the center of the inquiry.

We argue that the persistent scholarly disagreement over what SoC is and how it works is not a defect to be engineered away, but the most useful signal a computational method can capture about the form. We want to test the current understanding of SoC against the very texts that informed it, to find where our scholarly theories hold and where they blur. Our chapter makes two arguments that build on each other. First, we treat Cohn's mediation scale as something that can be applied consistently across an entire body of work, not just invoked in a single close reading, and ask what that consistency reveals about how modernist prose actually distributes itself along the scale, novel by novel and author by author. Second, and this is where Woolf becomes not just our test case but our central subject, we ask whether Woolf's fiction has a single position on that scale at all, or whether her reputation for a settled, recognizable "quiet" technique is itself a critical simplification that a passage-by-passage measurement can correct. These two arguments rely on a particular kind of computational method: large language models used not as classifiers producing a single confident verdict, but as resampled, independently reasoning readers whose agreement and disagreement are themselves data, analyzed with the same statistical caution a psychometrician would bring to any panel of raters.

Our corpus centers on Virginia Woolf's ten novels,  *The Voyage Out*, *Night and Day*, *Jacob's Room*, *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *Orlando*, *The Waves*, *Flush*, *The Years*, and *Between the Acts*,  alongside a comparative set spanning James Joyce, Dorothy Richardson, William Faulkner, Henry James, and Ford Madox Ford: twenty-one titles, six authors, published between 1902 and 1941\. Woolf presents a useful test case for our questions because of her own ambiguous relationship to SoC, both as a genre and as a set of techniques. Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of SoC narration, but, as Dora Zhang has counterintuitively noted, "despite the ubiquitous familiarity of \[SoC\] in general, and its association with Woolf in particular, her innovations in this arena have not been clearly recognized" (133). Woolf is thus highly legible for understanding the categories of SoC generally, and she is perhaps also in need of further analysis that acknowledges the complex undecidability of her SoC technique. Indeed, crafting classificatory and interpretive ambiguity is one of Woolf's primary aims: to create fiction that exposes "the limits of what we can know of other people" as well as the way "we read each other according to such predetermined categories" (Zhang 141). For Woolf, this is always a writerly problem as much as it is a social or representational one, and her SoC intricately encodes the limits of determinacy in its very movements between narrative modes. A scale built to measure exactly that movement, rather than to name it, is the right instrument for a writer whose subject is partly the instability of naming another mind at all.

**Woolf's Stream of Consciousness as Genre or Technique**

It is widely agreed that SoC is not a single narrative technique but an entire literary genre and subject matter, an overarching project for representing individual consciousness. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in their introduction to a special issue on SoC, "a definitive definition of the elusive label 'stream of consciousness' is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized" (2). This often manifests when we attempt to account for SoC as a total category, rather than focus specifically on particular authors' modes of representing the "stream" of experience and the mind. Bowler and Drewery emphasize the irreducibility of the SoC genre across authors and texts, that it cannot be boiled down to one thing: "all stream of consciousness writing is formally specific only to itself, but there has been a tendency to classify all such writing as adhering to the same technique or single form" (3). Despite this understanding, SoC undeniably has unique formal and syntactic characteristics that distinguish it from other narrative modes. Jan Mieszkowski describes SoC primarily through its sentential innovations: as a syntactic structure in addition to a theme. He writes that, in comparison to more traditional forms of representing consciousness like interior monologue, SoC renders interiority "verbally as strings of words that may or may not respect conventional grammatical, logical, or rhetorical paradigms … leveling or at least reconfiguring the traditional hierarchies ordering the parts of a sentence" (169-70). The linguistic, structural, formal understanding of SoC means that it is, at least in part, an identifiable set of techniques, even if no single named technique exhausts it. Franco Moretti even argues that the "intensity" of SoC in some texts "can actually be 'measured'" according to the frequency of each passage's "formal characteristics, parataxis, random associations, incorrect grammar, extension of the present moment," which "fluctuate continually" over the course of various passages (171). SoC is always made up of formal choices as well as thematic ones, and those choices vary continuously rather than snapping between discrete states,  which is precisely Cohn's point, made two decades before a computational method existed that could act on it directly.

Woolf's position in this conversation is interesting because she is widely recognized as an innovator of the SoC technique, yet her particular contribution to the form is undertheorized. We argue that three particular assumptions about SoC's techniques contribute to the discrepancy between Woolf's long-standing association with the rise of SoC and our particularized understanding of her version of it. First, Woolf's relative adherence to grammatical sentence structure makes her writing seem more constructed rather than organic, which resists conceptions of SoC as something that renders thought "naturally" on the page; second, Woolf as a SoC innovator has historically come second to Joyce, which has perpetuated the narrative that she is slightly less innovative when compared to the standards set by Joyce's own SoC techniques; and third, that her predominant use of free indirect discourse leads to the assumption that her narrative technique is more conventional. These assumptions, as they appear in scholarship, are interrelated; they all boil down to the idea that there is a standard, and more innovative, narrative about the development of SoC as a technique that Woolf does not quite adhere to.

Originating from William James's description of human thought as "flow" rather than "jointed"-ness, the metaphor of the "stream" of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. Novelist May Sinclair first imported the term into literature when reviewing the first three novel chapters of Dorothy Richardson's *Pilgrimage*, and with it the term's metaphorical associations of flow, "moments of … consciousness pass\[ing\] one by one" (58). Though scholars and practitioners of SoC themselves have complicated this understanding and taken issue with it, its origins in psychology have predetermined its association with both unformedness and single-mindedness. Such an understanding puts Woolf in the back seat, for "while \[SoC\] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom's soliloquy at the end of *Ulysses*, Woolf's innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly" (Zhang 133). Franco Moretti describes, for instance, Woolf's narratorial control in *To the Lighthouse*: "If, at certain moments, Lily Briscoe is seized by panic and on the point of losing control, Woolf's syntax is able to check any such drift, and give form to chaos … Nothing ungrammatical, here; no leveling parataxis, or random associations. In sentences woven into a lucid sequence leading towards their goal … narrative consciousness has regained the upper hand" (172). The standard against which Woolf's syntax is evaluated is "chaos"; Moretti's description of Woolf is written in negations, as he notes an expected style that is *absent* in the SoC passage. Anne Fernihough makes a version of the same comparison explicit, arguing that "Joyce's technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf's, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the *chaos* of the inner life" (66, emphasis in original), and David Dowling notes Woolf's relative "control" over even her most wandering passages: "Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a 'tidy mind' compared with that of Joyce" (48).

It is exactly this vocabulary of chaos and control that Cohn's scale was designed to replace with something measurable. In *Transparent Minds*, Cohn associates Woolf closely with what she calls "narrated monologue," a "character's mental discourse in the guise of the narrator's discourse," and a close analog to free indirect discourse (14). This technique, she writes, "needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction" (115). In other words, Woolf's primary choice of SoC technique was less a clean break from tradition and more a subtle shift, situated closer to the mediated end of Cohn's continuum than the "rival technique of the unsignaled 'Joycean' monologue" that anchors the other end (112). But narrated monologue is a *position* on a scale, not a fixed identity, and nothing in Cohn's account requires that a writer associated with it stay there. Dora Zhang's chapter convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionizes free indirect discourse more than others have previously acknowledged; it is Woolf's continued association with a single named technique, one position on a much longer scale, that has put closer analysis of her range on pause.

Taken together, these three assumptions describe a Woolf who is legible to SoC criticism only by comparison, and only ever in the negative: measured against a chaos she declines to produce, a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, and a free indirect discourse so familiar it no longer seems to require close technical scrutiny. This comparative frame, we suggest, has obscured a more basic question that the field has not yet put to Woolf's fiction directly: not how her stream of consciousness differs from Joyce's, but where, exactly, and how consistently, her prose sits on the single continuous scale that both writers' techniques share, novel by novel and even passage by passage. Answering that question requires more than another close reading of a single passage, however skillful; it requires applying Cohn's scale across an entire body of work, consistently, at a resolution fine enough to see whether "quiet" describes one style or several. That is the test to which we now turn.

**How the Passages Were Scored**

Before turning to what the readings show, it is worth pausing on what "reading" meant here in practice, since the method only supports the claims that follow if the reader understands, concretely, what produced a given passage's score.

Two systems did almost all of the reading this chapter draws on: GPT-5.4, OpenAI's most capable general-purpose model at the time of this study, and Qwen3-Max, the equivalent flagship system built by the Chinese technology company Alibaba. We chose two systems from two different companies, built on different underlying training data and different design philosophies, on purpose: if both, independently, tend to agree about where a passage sits, that agreement is harder to dismiss as one company's house style than if we had simply asked the same system the same question twice. A handful of passages, eleven in total, scattered across *Ulysses*, *The Sound and the Fury*, and *The Ambassadors*, were never read by Qwen3-Max at all, because Alibaba's own automatic content filters refused to process them; rather than quietly drop those passages from the corpus, we had them read instead by a third, smaller system, a version of the same underlying Qwen technology that we ran ourselves on university computing resources, so that no passage went missing from the record because of a commercial filter's judgment about its content rather than ours. A further, smaller round of scoring, described below where it becomes relevant, added two more independently-run systems, built by Meta and by the French company Mistral, to a subset of the corpus, as a check on whether the picture changes again with yet another pair of unrelated readers.

Both GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max belong to a class of systems sometimes called "reasoning models," built to work through a problem in writing before answering it. Given a passage, the system does not simply output a number; it produces a paragraph or more of visible deliberation first, considering the passage's grammar, weighing more than one possible reading, and only then committing to a placement on Cohn's scale. This is not a hidden process we have to infer from the outside. We can read it directly, the way a teacher might ask a student to show their work on an exam question rather than simply circle an answer, and in fact we required it: a system that returned only a bare number between 0 and 3 would tell us nothing about why it placed a passage there, and would give us no way to check whether that placement was a considered judgment or a coin flip that happened to land on a plausible-looking value.

Concretely, for every passage in the corpus, we gave each reader the passage itself, quoted exactly as it appears in the novel, along with a plain-language description of Cohn's four levels — not just "0 through 3" but a description of what each position on the scale means, illustrated with an example. We then asked the reader to do four things: decide whether the passage renders consciousness at all, as opposed to ordinary external narration or dialogue; if it does, place it on the scale; name which of Humphrey's older, more familiar technique-names it most resembles, as a secondary point of reference rather than the primary judgment; and, crucially, justify that placement using specific wording quoted from the passage itself, not a general impression of its style. We also required each reader to name the next-most-plausible alternative placement and explain why it rejected that alternative in favor of the one it chose — modeled on the way a graduate seminar asks a reader to address the strongest available counter-reading, not simply assert their own. A judgment that cannot survive stating the other side, in our view, is not yet a finding.

Because any single reading, human or machine, might be a fluke, no passage was scored only once. Each reader read and scored the same passage multiple times, independently of its own earlier attempts: three times across the corpus as a whole, and ten times for the small number of passages, like those in "Time Passes," discussed below, that we wanted to examine especially closely. A reader that gives the same reasoned answer nine times out of ten is doing something different from a reader that gives five different answers to the same passage across ten tries; the second case is closer to genuine uncertainty than to a stable judgment, and repeated, independent readings are what let us tell the two apart — the same distinction a critic would want to draw between a colleague who reliably argues for the same interpretation of a passage on repeated readings and one who is, in effect, guessing anew each time.

Two examples make this concrete. The opening of *To the Lighthouse* renders James Ramsay's joy at the prospect of a boat trip through an unmistakably interpretive, generalizing narrator: "since he belonged, even at the age of six, to that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that … James Ramsay, sitting on the floor cutting out pictures from the illustrated catalogue of the Army and Navy Stores, endowed the picture of a refrigerator as his mother spoke with heavenly bliss." Both readers, independently, placed this passage at level 0, psycho-narration, the most mediated point on the scale, and both grounded that placement in the same feature of the prose. GPT-5.4 wrote that "the passage clearly renders James's consciousness, but it does so through an authoritative narrator who explains and generalizes his mental process rather than presenting thought in his own idiom," citing the phrase "that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that" as evidence that the sentence's language belongs to the narrator's analytic vocabulary, not the six-year-old's. Qwen3-Max, reasoning independently, reached the identical placement by citing the identical kind of evidence: "these are analytic, classificatory statements about James's psychology, not direct access to his thought process," pointing to the same clause. Two systems, asked the same question about the same sentence without seeing each other's answers, converged not only on a score but on which words in the passage justified it.

A passage from *The Waves* shows what happens further along the scale, and previews a distinction this chapter returns to throughout. Louis, hiding from the other children, thinks: "I am on the other side of the hedge … O Lord, let them pass … I am green as a yew tree in the shade of the hedge. My hair is made of leaves. I am rooted to the middle of the earth. My body is a stalk." Here, every reader — GPT-5.4, Qwen3-Max, and, in the later round, the Meta-built system as well — placed the passage at level 3, autonomous monologue, agreeing that the unbroken first-person present tense, with no narratorial frame of any kind, put it at the least mediated extreme of the scale. But the readers did not agree on what to call it in Humphrey's older vocabulary. Qwen3-Max and the Meta-built system both named it a straightforward case of direct interior monologue. GPT-5.4 insisted instead on a hybrid label, arguing that the vegetal imagery — "my hair is made of leaves," "my body is a stalk" — was "too central to be reduced to incidental figurative language," that the boy's panic was being rendered *through* the metaphor of becoming a hedge rather than merely decorated by it, and so the passage was doing two things classifiable techniques disagree about, a case where naming resolves a family disagreement about categories that only pure identification cannot. This is a pattern that recurs throughout the corpus: readers converge tightly on *where* a passage sits on Cohn's continuous scale far more often than they converge on what discrete name to give that position, which is one reason this chapter treats the scale, not the label, as the more trustworthy measurement — and it is the reasoning traces themselves, not just the numbers, that let us see this happening in specific, quotable language rather than infer it from statistics alone.

**Measuring Mediation at Scale**

Cohn's scale is not, on its own, a computational instrument. Applying it to a single passage is an act of close reading; applying it consistently to eleven thousand passages across twenty-one novels requires a method that can hold a stable rubric across an enormous number of individual interpretive judgments without simply averaging away the disagreement that any two careful readers would have about some of them. Robert Humphrey's earlier taxonomy — the four broad techniques of presentation (direct interior monologue, indirect interior monologue, description by an omniscient author, soliloquy) elaborated through devices like free association, sensory image, and space-montage — was our first attempt at this problem, and it remains present in our data as a secondary label attached to every passage, in the way described above. But a typology of named types turns out to be the wrong instrument for the question this chapter actually wants to ask. Named categories force a binary judgment — does this passage belong to type X, yes or no — on a phenomenon that Cohn, and the tradition of narrated-monologue scholarship she inaugurated (see McHale, revisiting and refining Cohn's categories a generation later), had already described as continuous. Cohn's own scale is the more direct instrument, and it is the one the statistics below are built on.

The figures that follow are drawn from the full corpus: 11,952 passages and 72,167 individual (passage, reader, run) judgments, with every passage read by GPT-5.4 and, with the eleven-passage exception described above, by Qwen3-Max as well, each at three repeated runs. The later, smaller round that added the Meta- and Mistral-built readers extends a subset of this corpus rather than replacing it, and is identified as such wherever it is the source of a specific claim below.

Before trusting a score of this kind, we needed to know whether it was tracking something real about the prose or simply reproducing whatever a language model's prior notion of "stream of consciousness" already was. We ran two independent checks. The first tests mediation level against a small set of features computed without reading for meaning at all: the density of first-person pronouns, the ratio of past to non-past tense, and the density of internal punctuation (dashes, ellipses, semicolons). If the scale were merely an artifact of model idiosyncrasy, there is no reason these counts should predict it. They do: passages with a higher proportion of first-person language score reliably lower on the mediation scale, closer to the character's own voice, and passages with a higher proportion of past-tense narration score reliably higher, closer to the narrator's, both relationships strong and significant across a sampled validation set. One prediction made going in,  that heavier dash-and-ellipsis punctuation would track *lower* mediation, the visual signature of a mind interrupting itself,  ran backward: passages dense with any kind of internal punctuation actually skew toward more mediated, more narratorially controlled prose, consistent with this project's own earlier observation that Woolf's elaborate, subordinated syntax pulls toward psycho-narration rather than away from it. A specific, correctable prediction failing for an identifiable reason is, if anything, more reassuring than a set of results that had simply confirmed every expectation going in.

The second check answers a sharper worry, one specific to canonical modernism: that a model asked how mediated a *Mrs Dalloway* or *Ulysses* passage is may be retrieving the critical consensus about that novel rather than reading the passage itself. Large language models are demonstrably capable of reproducing well-known public-domain texts closely enough to raise exactly this concern, and *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *The Waves*, *Ulysses*, and Faulkner's two novels are among the most-discussed works in the corpus. We tested this directly by masking character names and other title-identifying features in a stratified sample of 177 passages from six of these titles and rescoring the masked versions in a blind fashion. If the model were substantially retrieving the book's reputation rather than reading its grammar, masking should produce large, inconsistent shifts. It does not: masked scores correlate with original scores at 0.902 across the sample, with a mean shift of \-0.042 on the 0–3 scale and a mean *absolute* shift of only 0.253,  noise, not a systematic collapse. The correlation is not uniform across titles (it is highest for *To the Lighthouse*, at 0.95, and lowest for *Mrs Dalloway*, at 0.74, itself a small, specific finding worth further attention rather than an average to be trusted blindly), but nowhere does it suggest the scale is mostly reading the byline. Taken together, these two checks do not prove that a mediation score is equivalent to a trained narratologist's judgment,  two large language models sharing similar training data and similar failure modes is not the same thing as two independent human coders, and we do not claim otherwise,  but they do show that the number answers to the passage's own grammar and content, not to a model's untethered impression of what a famous novel ought to sound like.

**Author Fingerprints Across the Corpus**

With the scale validated, the first question is the simplest: does the mediation level, averaged across an author's entire output, differ from author to author in a way that aligns with anything criticism would recognize? It does. Faulkner's two novels average 2.07 on the 0–3 scale, with a spread (standard deviation) of 1.23 and fifty-nine percent of all his scored passages landing at the least mediated extreme, autonomous monologue,  the profile of a writer whose SoC prose oscillates between very little mediation and quite a lot of it, rarely settling in the middle. Henry James sits at the opposite pole: a mean of 0.44, a spread of only 0.58, and sixty percent of his passages at the most mediated level, psycho-narration,  a narrator who reports on consciousness from outside and rarely relinquishes that position, which is exactly the role James's fiction is usually assigned in accounts of the novel's prehistory to stream of consciousness proper. Joyce, the writer against whom Woolf's supposed quietness is conventionally measured, averages 1.37, roughly a third of his passages at each of level 0, level 1, and level 3, a genuinely bimodal-leaning profile rather than a settled one. Dorothy Richardson, whose *Pilgrimage* volumes are usually grouped with Woolf as the other major "technique" writer of the group, averages 0.91 with a comparatively tight spread of 0.71, sixty-one percent of her passages clustering specifically at narrated monologue, the most concentrated single-level profile of any author in the corpus.

And Woolf: a mean of 0.82, a spread of 0.88, forty-three percent of her passages at psycho-narration and forty percent at narrated monologue, with only seventeen percent reaching quoted or autonomous monologue at all. On the numbers alone, this places her prose closer to Richardson's and James's than to Faulkner's or, on this measure, even Joyce's, as far as it goes, evidence for exactly the "quiet" reputation the field has assigned her. Joyce's own developmental arc across the corpus's three Joyce titles confirms a version of the standard account on its own terms: *Dubliners* (1914) averages 0.38, *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* (1916) averages 0.69, and *Ulysses* (1922) averages 1.77, a monotonic march toward less mediation that matches the way literary history usually tells Joyce's story, one novel's technique building on the last.

But an author-level mean, however well validated, describes only the center of a distribution, and the word "quiet" has always implied not just where Woolf's prose sits on average but that it sits there consistently, novel after novel, in a way that would make her body of work legible as a single style. That claim is testable independently of the mean, and it is where the numbers stop confirming the standard account.

**Woolf's Variation, Novel to Novel**

The reception history this chapter has been describing treats Woolf's SoC as, in effect, a fixed style: quieter than Joyce's, more grammatically composed, essentially the same technique deployed across a career. Cohn's scale lets us test that assumption directly, not by asking how Woolf compares to other authors on average, but by asking how much a given Woolf novel's mean mediation score differs from the mean of her other novels,  whether "Woolf's SoC" is one thing measured ten times, or ten things that happen to share a name.

It is closer to the second. Using an intraclass correlation (ICC),  a variance-components measure of between-title consistency that corrects for the fact that an author with more titles in a sample is otherwise structurally advantaged relative to an author with fewer,  Woolf's ten novels score 0.561, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of \[0.534, 0.588\]: more than half of the total variation in mediation level across her corpus falls between books rather than within them. No other author in the comparative set comes close on titles that are genuinely independent works. Joyce scores 0.358 \[0.322, 0.391\]; Faulkner, on only two titles, scores 0.037 \[0.006, 0.085\]; Henry James scores 0.008 \[0.000, 0.022\]. Richardson scores 0.001, essentially zero, but this needs an immediate caveat rather than a footnote: *Pilgrimage*'s volumes are installments of one continuous narrative rather than independent novels, so near-zero between-volume variance is close to a definitional fact about serial fiction, not a finding about a fixed authorial style, and we do not use her as a comparison case on this axis for that reason.

It would be easy to assume this entire result is *The Waves* doing all the work, and a large part of it is: leave-one-title-out sensitivity shows that removing *The Waves* alone drops Woolf's ICC from 0.561 to 0.199. That is a real and important qualification. It is not, however, the whole story, for two reasons. First, 0.199 is still higher than Faulkner's or James's full-corpus figures, and higher than Joyce's own score after removing *his* single most extreme title, *Ulysses*, which falls to 0.117 once excluded; Woolf minus her most unusual novel is still more variable, book to book, than Joyce minus his. Second, removing any of Woolf's other nine titles individually moves the ICC only slightly, from 0.563 (without *Flush*) to 0.612 (without *The Years*), meaning that whatever is driving Woolf's between-book variance beyond *The Waves* is distributed across her career rather than concentrated in some second outlier. Nor is the finding an artifact of including *Flush* and *Orlando*, Woolf's two genre-heterogeneous mock-biographies, in a sample otherwise composed of more conventional novels; excluding both actually raises Woolf's ICC to 0.586. However the calculation is sliced, two claims survive: *The Waves* is genuinely, measurably discontinuous with the rest of Woolf's fiction, more discontinuous than any single title's departure from any other author's baseline in this corpus; and the other nine novels, considered on their own, still vary by book more than a "settled style" account predicts.

A mean, though, discards exactly the thing narratologists are usually most interested in about free indirect discourse: not a passage's average distance from the narrator, but its *movement*, the sentence-by-sentence oscillation between voices that gives the technique its characteristic texture. A mean of 0.87 for *Mrs Dalloway* is compatible with prose that alternates violently between levels 0 and 2, or with prose that sits flatly at level 1 throughout,  and criticism has generally assumed the former. The full within-title distributions, and a measure of how strongly one passage's level predicts the next passage's level (a lag-1 autocorrelation along the text), let us ask directly which picture is closer to true, novel by novel.

The answer varies enough to complicate the mean-level story on its own terms. *Mrs Dalloway* (mean 0.89) is in fact the most internally concentrated of Woolf's mid-range novels: seventy-four percent of its passages land at narrated monologue specifically, with only seven percent reaching level 2 and one percent level 3, a narrow, mid-scale band rather than an oscillation between extremes. *To the Lighthouse* (mean 0.76) looks similar: sixty-seven percent at narrated monologue, only four percent above it. *The Years* (mean 0.98), despite an almost identical mean to *Mrs Dalloway*, has a visibly different shape: twenty-seven percent at level 0, forty-nine percent at level 1, and a full twenty-four percent at level 2,  a book that reaches further up the scale far more often than *Dalloway* does, even though the two novels' averages would suggest near-identical technique. *Jacob's Room* (mean 0.59) splits almost evenly between levels 0 and 1 (fifty-one and thirty-nine percent), a genuinely bimodal-leaning profile that its middling mean conceals entirely. *The Waves* alone, as expected, sits overwhelmingly at the far end: sixty-seven percent of its passages at autonomous monologue, only four percent at psycho-narration, the closest thing in the entire Woolf corpus to Faulkner's profile rather than James's.

Autocorrelation adds a further, and in some ways more surprising, axis of variation, one no account of Woolf's "quiet" style has had the resolution to notice before. *Between the Acts* (0.366), *Orlando* (0.325), and *To the Lighthouse* (0.315) show the strongest tendency for a passage's mediation level to predict the next passage's level,  registers that, once entered, tend to be sustained for a stretch of text, comparable in strength to Faulkner's *As I Lay Dying* (0.327) and well above most of Richardson's *Pilgrimage* volumes. *Flush* (0.050) and *Jacob's Room* (0.051), by contrast, sit near the floor of the entire corpus, indistinguishable in this respect from Henry James's most narratorially stable novel, *The Wings of the Dove* (0.022): register that moves passage to passage almost independently of what came before, regardless of a mean that, in *Jacob's Room*'s case, is itself already the most volatile-by-distribution of any Woolf novel below *The Waves*. *Mrs Dalloway* (0.177) and *The Years* (0.164) land in between, closer to the low end. What this shows is that "how consistently a Woolf novel holds its register" and "how mediated a Woolf novel is on average" are not the same variable and do not move together: *Between the Acts* is both comparatively mediated and comparatively sustained in that mediation, holding a chosen register for a stretch of prose at a time; *Jacob's Room* is comparatively unmediated on average and almost entirely unpredictable passage to passage, its narrator's distance from Jacob's mind resetting with each new paragraph rather than settling into a discernible pattern. Two novels can share a mean and differ completely in texture, or share a texture and differ in mean; Woolf's ten novels do both, in different combinations, novel by novel.

Two further observations belong here, both of which cut against a career-arc reading of these results. *The Waves* (1931) sits chronologically between *Orlando* (1928) and *Flush* (1933), two of the most heavily mediated novels in Woolf's output; the departure is not the culmination of a steady drift toward less mediation across her career, but a single, bounded event, bracketed on both sides by a return to something closer to her earlier register. And across the full twenty-one-title corpus, a passage-level test for a diachronic trend toward less classifiable, more taxonomy-resistant prose over the 1902–1941 span found no reliable trend at all (Spearman's rho \= 0.104, p \= 0.655): whatever is happening across Woolf's career, it is not part of a broader modernist drift that a mere publication date predicts. If Woolf's fiction has a shape, it is not a line moving toward one destination; it is closer to a set of discrete, revisited choices, of which *The Waves* is the one time she declined the choice she otherwise kept making.

**Close Reading at Scale: "Time Passes"**

The corpus-wide results speak to Woolf's SoC across whole novels; it is worth also asking what the same method can do at the scale of a single, already-famous crux. "Time Passes," the short middle section of *To the Lighthouse* in which the Ramsays' house stands empty for ten years and Mrs Ramsay's death is disposed of in a single bracketed aside, is a canonical site of exactly this kind of narratorial strangeness in Woolf criticism, a section critics have long treated as doing something formally unusual, without always agreeing on what. It is also, in our data, the single site in two full novels' worth of scored passages where a reader's antimimetic flag ,  reserved for a passage that resists placement on Cohn's scale altogether, for an explicit textual reason, not merely low confidence,  actually fires. That made it worth a properly powered second look: rescoring all thirty-three passages of the section at ten runs per reader instead of three, rather than resting an interpretive claim on a single low-probability hit.

"Time Passes" is famous for its strangeness, and the natural assumption going in was that strangeness would register as *less* mediated prose: thought breaking further free of narratorial control, the mind rendered rawer and closer to its own voice, as in Faulkner's or Joyce's more radical passages. It turns out to be the opposite. Across the section, mediation scores skew markedly toward the heavily mediated end of the scale compared to the novel's baseline, and the great majority of its stable, SoC-positive passages land at psycho-narration, the *most* mediated point on Cohn's scale, not the least. Whatever is strange about "Time Passes," then, is not that Woolf's narrator recedes; it is that the narrator's ordinary move, interpreting a mind from outside, "she felt," "it seemed as if," keeps firing on content that increasingly has no mind, in any ordinary sense, to interpret.

That displacement sits in almost no space at all: two sentences, out of thirty-three passages, in which mental predicates are given not to a character but to something that is not a person. In one, "certain airs, detached from the body of the wind," are described entering the house "questioning and wondering," asking, "Were they allies? Were they enemies?" In the other, the room's own silence is personified the same way, "weaving" the cries of birds and the drone of the fields into itself and "folding them round the house in silence," the same rendering of thought-like agency in something that has no mind to think it. On these two sentences, and on almost no others in the whole sample, our two most consistent readers, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max, come apart in a way that is not noise. Across ten repeated readings of the first sentence, GPT-5.4 does not scatter across many answers; it lands, almost evenly, on two distinct positions, each independently reproduced: five readings that hold the passage on the scale, under strain, as an unusually mediated simulation of a state of mind, and five that push it off the scale altogether, arguing that thought-like inquiry without a "plausible single consciousness to own it" cannot be assigned a mediation level built on the assumption that rendered consciousness has a human owner. Qwen3-Max, reading the identical sentence, registers almost no strain at all: nine of ten readings place it confidently at psycho-narration, the personified airs treated as ordinary narratorial imagery rather than as anything the scale cannot hold.

This is, in miniature, close to the theoretical debate Monika Fludernik and, following her, Jan Alber and Brian Richardson have had about "unnatural" narrative technique more broadly: whether devices that seem to exceed realist consciousness-representation are a genuinely distinct category, or whether they are simply conventions not yet naturalized by a given reader's expectations. Our data does not answer that debate, but it does show it actually happening in a specific, quotable pair of sentences, between two readers with different priors about how far a figure of speech can carry an interior state before it stops being a figure and starts being a problem for the scale itself. It also lets us say something more precise than "Woolf's SoC sometimes exceeds measurement": across the whole of two novels, this device,  thought-like predicates attached to a non-human antecedent,  is the one place, nearly the only place, where whether Cohn's scale holds at all depends on which reader, human or machine, is doing the holding.

**Restraint as a Repeated Choice**

This data does not yield a corrected measure of how quiet Woolf's SoC really is relative to Joyce's, nor a definitive answer to whether she belongs among literature's more or less radical stream-of-consciousness writers, and it would be a mistake to read it that way. The comparative frame this chapter opened by describing,  Woolf measured against a chaos she declines, a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt,  turns out to survive at the level of the author-wide mean: on average, across her whole output, Woolf's prose really is more mediated than Joyce's or Faulkner's, more concentrated at psycho-narration and narrated monologue, closer to Richardson's profile than to theirs. What does not survive is the assumption bundled inside that comparison, that the mean describes a *style*, something Woolf does consistently, novel after novel, the way "chaos" is assumed to be something Joyce does consistently. Nine of her ten novels occupy a band on Cohn's scale that is far wider and far more internally varied, in both level and texture, than a single adjective like "quiet" can hold; the tenth, *The Waves*, leaves that band entirely, more radically unmediated by our measure than either of Faulkner's novels, and does so as a single bounded event in the middle of her career rather than its culmination. The right description of Woolf's stream of consciousness, on this evidence, is not a fixed point on a scale running from control to chaos, but a record of a writer choosing restraint, repeatedly, in different ways in different books, and once, deliberately, choosing not to.

This is also what a computational method can contribute to a problem that close reading alone has spent a century worrying at without resolving. It was never going to settle whether SoC is a genre or a technique, or produce a scale finer than Cohn's that finally holds without remainder. Two large language models trained on similar data and prone to similar failure modes are not independent coders in the sense inter-rater reliability normally requires, and a computational finding, however well validated against surface features and blind to a novel's title, still answers a question a critic posed to it rather than discovering the question on its own. But the method can apply an existing, theoretically grounded scale with total consistency across an entire body of work, at a resolution no reader could sustain unaided across twenty-one novels and eleven thousand passages; it can locate, with precision, where that consistency is real and where it is a mean concealing real variation underneath; and it can follow the one place the scale itself breaks down all the way to two sentences and a disagreement each reader can actually articulate. Long and So were right that everyone knows stream of consciousness when they see it. This chapter's contribution is a way of seeing, at the scale of a career rather than a passage, that what Woolf's readers have been calling one style was several, and that the moment she left all of them behind can be dated, measured, and read closely, on the same page.
