Virginia Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of stream-of-consciousness (SOC) narration, the ever-familiar term in modernist studies—and modern fiction generally—for literary works which foreground the idiosyncratic movements of the mind as it attempts to render the intricacies of experience. Yet scholars are sometimes hard pressed to identify the ways Woolf’s particular strain of SOC breaks from tradition, finding ambiguity and blurriness in her use of accepted SOC technique, as well as surprising adherence to older modes of narration where more radical expression might be expected. When it comes to SOC form, Woolf’s “innovations … have not been clearly recognized” because, perhaps, her fiction contains less obvious markers of innovative narrative technique: “while \[SOC\] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom’s soliloquy at the end of *Ulysses*, Woolf’s innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly” (Zhang 133). We argue that this gap exposes a need for a different formal criteria for recognizing Woolf’s unique narrative experimentation, one which considers SOC on a *scale of narrative mediation* rather than as a binaristic set of techniques such as those listed by Zhang and other SOC scholars. Through a computational, LLM-based methodology drawn from Dorrit Cohn’s concept of levels of mediation, we suggest that Woolf’s ambiguous position in the history and development of SOC comes from a continual, yet constantly shifting, book-by-book experimentation with narrative modes. 

Arriving at a clearer sense of Woolf’s particular mode of SOC involves stripping back some of the assumptions about her writing that have persisted since early modernist scholarship. Woolf is generally thought to have a “more quiet” expression of SOC than some of her contemporaries, particularly James Joyce, who often defines the more radical strain of modernist narrative experimentation. Many scholars describe Woolf’s SOC through a comparison with Joyce. Anne Fernihough argues that “Joyce's technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf's, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the *chaos* of the inner life” (66, emphasis in original), and David Dowling, drawing from David Daiches, notes Woolf's relative “control” over even her most wandering passages: “Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a ‘tidy mind’ compared with that of Joyce” (48). Overall, one gets the impression that Woolf’s narration surprisingly does *not* include the standard techniques of other modernist writing. For instance, Franco Moretti describes Woolf’s narratorial control in *To the Lighthouse* through negations: 

If, at certain moments, Lily Briscoe is seized by panic and on the point of losing control, Woolf's syntax is able to check any such drift, and give form to chaos … Nothing ungrammatical, here; no leveling parataxis, or random associations. In sentences woven into a lucid sequence leading towards their goal … narrative consciousness has regained the upper hand. (172)

These critics evaluate Woolf’s “controlled” syntax against a particular experimental standard—“chaos”—rather than constructing a definition of SOC out of Woolf’s own instantiation of it. When scholars do pay closer attention to Woolf’s SOC technique specifically, it is closely associated with free indirect discourse, or similarly what Dorrit Cohn calls “narrated monologue,” a “character’s mental discourse in the guise of the narrator’s discourse” (14). This technique, Cohn writes, “needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction” (115). In other words, Woolf's primary choice of SOC technique was less a clean break from tradition and more a subtle shift, situated oppositely from the “rival technique of the unsignaled ‘Joycean’ monologue” (112). Dora Zhang convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionized free indirect discourse more than others have acknowledged, but still this perpetuates Woolf’s association with a single named technique.

Taken together, these assumptions describe a Woolf who is legible to SOC criticism only by comparison, and only ever in the negative: measured against a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, a chaos she declines to produce, and a free indirect discourse so familiar it no longer seems to require close technical scrutiny. This comparative frame, we suggest, has obscured a more basic question that the field has not yet put to Woolf's fiction directly: not how her stream of consciousness differs from Joyce's, but where, exactly, and how consistently, her prose sits on the single continuous scale that both writers' techniques share, novel by novel and even passage by passage. Answering that question requires more than another close reading of a single passage, however skillful; it requires applying that scale across an entire body of work, consistently, at a resolution fine enough to see whether “quiet” describes one style or several.

To approach such a question, our chapter makes two arguments that build on each other. First, we turn to Cohn’s mediation scale as something that can be applied consistently across an entire body of work, not just invoked in a single close reading, and ask what that consistency reveals about how modernist SOC prose actually distributes itself along the scale, novel by novel and author by author. Second, we evaluate Woolf’s specific relation to this larger corpus, asking whether her fiction has a single position on that scale at all, or whether her reputation for a settled, recognizable “quiet” technique is itself a critical simplification that a passage-by-passage measurement can correct. **\[give an actual preview into the conclusions we come to\].** These two arguments rely on a particular kind of computational method: large language models used not as classifiers producing a single confident verdict, but as resampled, independently reasoning readers whose agreement and disagreement are themselves data, analyzed with the same statistical caution a psychometrician would bring to any panel of raters.

**SOC, Dorrit Cohn’s Scale of Mediation, and Methodology**

Originating from William James's description of human thought and brought into literary studies by May Sinclair in a review of Dorothy Richardson’s novels, the metaphor of the "stream" of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. As a broad literary genre, SOC has come to describe works that “attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual” by showing “not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced" (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SOC’s particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. Many early attempts to resolve this issue have reached for a typology: a set of named categorical techniques—interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and so on—into which a passage of prose can be sorted. Robert Humphrey’s 1954 *Stream of Consciousness in the Modern Novel* is the foundational instance and remains one of the field’s most cited points of reference. While Humphrey acknowledges from the outset the tricky ambiguity of SOC as both genre and technique, he ultimately uses the book to attempt to delineate buckets of techniques into which passages can be sorted. 

Indeed, a typology built from named, discrete categories has a specific cost: it asks a passage to declare itself as one thing among several, when what critics (including Humphrey himself) have actually described is a matter of degree. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in their introduction to a special issue on SOC, “a definitive definition of the elusive label ‘stream of consciousness’ is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized” (2). They go on to take issue with the critical impulse historically to “classify” SOC based on these supposedly measurable categories: “all stream of consciousness writing is formally specific only to itself, but there has been a tendency to classify all such writing as adhering to the same technique or single form” (3). \[**more**\]

In her seminal narratological study of interiority in fiction, *Transparent Minds* (1978), Dorrit Cohn pushes back against the prevailing view that SOC was as “radical a break with tradition as has been assumed,” instead placing it within a broader discussion of how authors have chosen to mediate the distance between narrator and character (9). Rather than asking which of several techniques a SOC passage contains, Cohn asks a single, continuous question: how much mediation stands between the narrator’s voice and the character’s consciousness? Passages narrating character interiority are evaluated along a mediation scale running from “psycho-narration” (the narrator’s own account of a character’s inner life, from outside), though narrated monologue (the character’s thought rendered in the narrator’s own grammar, without quotation), to quoted monologue (an interior utterance set off, however lightly, as the character’s own words), and finally to autonomous monologue (thought presented with no narratorial frame at all). \[**more** on Cohn\] This chapter takes Cohn’s scale as its starting point and principal computational instrument, and asks what a large corpus of SOC prose looks like when read along it, resampled, at scale, by multiple independent readers, human and machine.
