Was Woolf Ever “Quiet”? A Computational Evaluation of Levels of Mediation in Stream of Consciousness Novels

Virginia Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of stream-of-consciousness (SOC) narration, the ever-familiar term in modernist studies—and modern fiction generally—for literary works which foreground the idiosyncratic movements of the mind as it attempts to render the intricacies of experience. Yet scholars are sometimes hard-pressed to identify the ways Woolf’s particular strain of SOC breaks from tradition, finding ambiguity and blurriness in her use of accepted SOC technique, as well as surprising adherence to older modes of narration where more radical expression might be expected. When it comes to SOC form, Woolf’s “innovations … have not been clearly recognized” because, perhaps, her fiction contains less obvious markers of innovative narrative technique: “while \[SOC\] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom’s soliloquy at the end of *Ulysses*, Woolf’s innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly” (Zhang 133). We argue that this gap exposes a need for a different formal criteria for recognizing Woolf’s unique narrative experimentation, one which considers SOC on a *scale of narrative mediation* rather than as a binaristic set of techniques such as those listed by Zhang and other SOC scholars. Through a computational, large language model (LLM) methodology drawn from Dorrit Cohn’s concept of levels of mediation, we suggest that Woolf’s ambiguous position in the history and development of SOC comes from a continual, yet constantly shifting, book-by-book experimentation with narrative modes. 

Arriving at a clearer sense of Woolf’s particular mode of SOC involves stripping back some of the assumptions about her writing that have persisted since early modernist scholarship. Woolf is generally thought to have a “more quiet” expression of SOC than some of her contemporaries, particularly James Joyce, who often defines the more radical strain of modernist narrative experimentation. Many scholars describe Woolf’s SOC through a comparison with Joyce. Anne Fernihough argues that “Joyce's technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf's, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the *chaos* of the inner life” (66, emphasis in original), and David Dowling, drawing from David Daiches, notes Woolf's relative “control” over even her most wandering passages: “Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a ‘tidy mind’ compared with that of Joyce” (48). Overall, one gets the impression that Woolf’s narration surprisingly does *not* include the standard techniques of other modernist writing. For instance, Franco Moretti describes Woolf’s narratorial control in *To the Lighthouse* through negations: 

If, at certain moments, Lily Briscoe is seized by panic and on the point of losing control, Woolf's syntax is able to check any such drift, and give form to chaos … Nothing ungrammatical, here; no leveling parataxis, or random associations. In sentences woven into a lucid sequence leading towards their goal … narrative consciousness has regained the upper hand. (172)

These critics evaluate Woolf’s “controlled” syntax against a particular experimental standard—“chaos”—rather than constructing a definition of SOC out of Woolf’s own instantiation of it. When scholars do pay closer attention to Woolf’s SOC technique specifically, it is closely associated with free indirect discourse, or similarly what Dorrit Cohn calls “narrated monologue,” a “character’s mental discourse in the guise of the narrator’s discourse” (14). This technique, Cohn writes, “needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction” (115). In other words, Woolf's primary choice of SOC technique was less a clean break from tradition and more a subtle shift, situated oppositely from the “rival technique of the unsignaled ‘Joycean’ monologue” (112). Dora Zhang convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionized free indirect discourse more than others have acknowledged, but still this perpetuates Woolf’s association with a single named technique.

Taken together, these assumptions describe a Woolf who is legible to SOC criticism only by comparison, and only ever in the negative: measured against a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, a chaos she declines to produce, and a free indirect discourse so familiar it no longer seems to require close technical scrutiny. This comparative frame, we suggest, has obscured a more basic question that the field has not yet put to Woolf's fiction directly: not how her stream of consciousness differs from Joyce's, but where, exactly, and how consistently, her prose sits on the single continuous scale that both writers' techniques share, novel by novel and even passage by passage. Answering that question requires more than another close reading of a single passage, however skillful; it requires applying that scale across an entire body of work, consistently, at a resolution fine enough to see whether “quiet” describes one style or several.

To approach such a question, our chapter makes two arguments that build on each other. First, we turn to Cohn’s mediation scale as something that can be applied consistently across an entire body of work, not just invoked in a single close reading, and ask what that consistency reveals about how modernist SOC prose actually distributes itself along the scale, novel by novel and author by author. Second, we evaluate Woolf’s specific relation to this larger corpus, asking whether her fiction has a single position on that scale at all, or whether her reputation for a settled, recognizable “quiet” technique is itself a critical simplification that a passage-by-passage measurement can correct. What we find is that the familiar comparison survives, but only as an average. Taken across her whole output, Woolf’s prose does sit nearer the narrator’s end of Cohn’s scale than Joyce’s or Faulkner’s. Yet more of the variation in her prose falls between one novel and the next than within any one of them, a larger share than for any other writer we examined, and that remains true even once *The Waves*, her most obvious departure, is set aside. “Quiet,” on this evidence, names an average rather than a style. These two arguments rely on a particular kind of computational method: large language models used not as classifiers producing a single confident verdict, but as resampled, independently reasoning readers whose agreement and disagreement are themselves data, analyzed with the same statistical caution a psychometrician would bring to any panel of raters.

Finally, this speaks to a larger paradox about SOC as a literary phenomenon: that despite its ubiquity it has been notoriously difficult to characterize concretely. Hoyt Long and Richard Jean So sum up the problem succinctly in their large-scale computational study of SOC narration across fictional traditions: “no one can agree on what \[SOC\] is, yet everyone seems to know it when they see it” (346). For such a widely-discussed literary phenomenon, it is striking that SOC continues to be undecidable in its particularities, and also that it endures as something which wants to be particularized. Perhaps, however, the persistent scholarly disagreement over what SOC is and how it works is not a defect to be engineered away, but the most useful signal a computational method can capture about the form. By shifting how we think about SOC, as a movement between genre—an overarching project seeking to accurately represent consciousness—and technique—a narrative tool with distinct and identifiable formal elements, we suggest that modernism’s premium on the new and the radical has led criticism to look for SOC as a break, when the record of these novels looks more like a range of positions that writers moved between, and back again. May Sinclair, who gave the term its literary currency in her 1918 review of Richardson, is a case in point: her own novels, narrated largely from the narrator’s side of the scale through *The Three Sisters* (1914), move markedly toward narrated monologue in *The Tree of Heaven* and *Mary Olivier* (1917–1919), in the very years she was naming the form.

**Reading Stream of Consciousness along Dorrit Cohn’s Scale of Mediation**

In order to apply a systematic computational method to the question of what defines Woolf’s particular mode of SOC, we had to decide how to differentiate modes of SOC in the first place. But this itself is still a major area of debate. Originating from William James's description of human thought and brought into literary studies by May Sinclair in a review of Dorothy Richardson’s novels, the metaphor of the "stream" of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. As a broad literary genre, SOC has come to describe works that “attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual” by showing “not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced" (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SOC’s particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. Many early attempts to resolve this issue have reached for a typology: a set of named categorical techniques—interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and so on—into which a passage of prose can be sorted. Robert Humphrey’s 1954 *Stream of Consciousness in the Modern Novel* is the foundational instance and remains one of the field’s most cited points of reference. While Humphrey acknowledges from the outset the tricky ambiguity of SOC as both genre and technique, he ultimately uses the book to attempt to delineate buckets of techniques into which passages can be sorted. 

Indeed, a typology built from named, discrete categories has a specific cost: it asks a passage to declare itself as one thing among several, when what critics (including Humphrey himself) have actually described is a matter of degree. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in their introduction to a special issue on SOC, “a definitive definition of the elusive label ‘stream of consciousness’ is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized” (2). They go on to take issue with the critical impulse historically to “classify” SOC based on these supposedly measurable categories: “all stream of consciousness writing is formally specific only to itself, but there has been a tendency to classify all such writing as adhering to the same technique or single form” (3). This “tendency” in part has to do with the idea that SOC in its purest form was a complete shift in how consciousness was portrayed in the novel: that true SOC completely altered standard modes of narration, through new syntactic structures that “may or may not respect conventional grammatical, logical, or rhetorical paradigms … leveling or at least reconfiguring the traditional hierarchies ordering the parts of a sentence” (Mieszkowski 169-70). Thinking of SOC as simply experimental technique forecloses the possibility of recognizing the myriad ways different authors deploy both traditional and new narrative modes toward the project of rendering consciousness.

We needed a way to appropriately measure SOC in all its fluidity—to pin down a concrete and precise formal way of measuring it while also not imposing historical assumptions about the form onto Woolf’s texts. In her seminal narratological study of interiority in fiction, *Transparent Minds* (1978), Dorrit Cohn pushes back against the prevailing view that SOC was as “radical a break with tradition as has been assumed,” instead placing it within a broader discussion of how authors have chosen to mediate the distance between narrator and character (9). Rather than asking which of several techniques a SOC passage contains, Cohn asks a single, continuous question: how much mediation stands between the narrator’s voice and the character’s consciousness? Passages narrating character interiority are evaluated along a mediation scale running from “psycho-narration” (the narrator’s own account of a character’s inner life, from outside), through narrated monologue (the character’s thought rendered in the narrator’s own grammar, without quotation), to quoted monologue (an interior utterance set off, however lightly, as the character’s own words), and finally to autonomous monologue (thought presented with no narratorial frame at all). We number these four positions from 0 to 3, from most mediated to least, so that throughout this chapter a higher number means that a character’s consciousness reaches the page with less of the narrator in between. Cohn’s scale offers a corrective to the classificatory impulse of SOC scholarship while also still providing a systematic framework that can be scaled up computationally.

*Measuring Mediation at Scale: Methodology and Verification*

This chapter takes Cohn’s scale as its starting point and principal computational instrument, and asks what a large corpus of SOC prose looks like when read along it, resampled, at scale, by multiple independent readers. Our corpus centers on Virginia Woolf's ten novels, *The Voyage Out*, *Night and Day*, *Jacob's Room*, *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *Orlando*, *The Waves*, *Flush*, *The Years*, and *Between the Acts*, alongside a comparative set of thirty-four works by six other writers, published between 1875 and 1941. Joyce (three works) is the comparison the reception history insists on. Dorothy Richardson (nine volumes of *Pilgrimage*) is the novelist whose work first drew the phrase “stream of consciousness” into literary criticism, and May Sinclair (eight novels) is the critic who put it there, in her 1918 review of *Pilgrimage*, before writing in the mode herself; hers is the only career in the corpus that crosses the coinage of the term. William Faulkner (three works) represents the form’s later, American extension. Henry James (six novels) and Ford Madox Ford (five) serve as a comparison set: novelists deeply concerned with rendering consciousness who are not usually counted among stream-of-consciousness writers. To extend Faulkner’s and Richardson’s careers beyond what is in the public domain, we added eight further works, five Faulkner novels from *Mosquitoes* (1927) to *Absalom, Absalom!* (1936) and the last three volumes of *Pilgrimage*, which we read with a smaller panel and use only where they bear on a specific claim about those two careers.

It is worth noting that preliminary versions of our research began with Humphrey’s typology instead of Cohn’s scale. We found that while Humphrey’s categories do describe particular techniques found repeatedly throughout SOC writing, they were simultaneously too broad and too binary to accurately be applied to full texts. Multiple rounds of testing produced highly ambiguous results: models were frequently unsure about what passage belonged to what category. A catch-all “hybrid” category was offered as a solution to passages which combined multiple techniques, but this made accurately measuring categorical distribution impossible. When we later expressed the same judgments in both vocabularies, the difficulty became measurable. Our two main readers chose the same one of Humphrey’s techniques for a passage only a little over half the time, while agreeing noticeably more often on its position on Cohn’s scale (Cohen’s κ of 0.39 for the techniques against 0.50 for the scale). More than four in ten of their disagreements over technique, moreover, fell at a single seam, between indirect interior monologue and omniscient description, which is to say at Cohn’s boundary between narrated monologue and psycho-narration: the very boundary on which critical accounts of Woolf have always turned.

For every passage in the corpus, we gave each of our two principal readers the passage itself, quoted exactly as it appears in the novel, along with a plain-language description of Cohn's four levels, not just “0 through 3,” but a description of what each position on the scale means, illustrated with an example. We then asked the reader to do four things: decide whether the passage renders consciousness at all, as opposed to ordinary external narration or dialogue; if it does, place it on the scale; name which of Humphrey's older, more familiar technique-names it most resembles, as a secondary point of reference rather than the primary judgment; and, crucially, justify that placement using specific wording quoted from the passage itself, not a general impression of its style. We also required each reader to name the next-most-plausible alternative placement and explain why it rejected that alternative in favor of the one it chose and modeled on the way a seminar asks a reader to address the strongest available counter-reading, not simply assert their own. A judgment that cannot survive stating the other side, in our view, is not yet a finding. Because any single reading, human or machine, might be a fluke, each passage was scored several times. Each of the two readers read and scored every passage three times across the corpus as a whole, and ten times for the thirty-three passages of “Time Passes,” which we examine closely in a later section of this chapter. 

We should note exactly what “reading” means here in practice. Two LLMs did almost all of the reading this chapter draws on: GPT-5.4, OpenAI’s most capable general-purpose model at the time of this study, and Qwen3-Max, the equivalent flagship system built by Alibaba. We chose two systems from two different companies, built on different underlying training data and different design philosophies, on purpose: if both, independently, tend to agree about where a passage sits, that agreement is harder to dismiss as one model’s bias than if we had simply asked the same system the same question twice. A handful of passages, nine in total, scattered across *Ulysses*, *The Sound and the Fury*, *The Ambassadors*, *What Maisie Knew*, and *The Last Post*, were never read by Qwen3-Max at all, because Alibaba’s own automatic content filters refused to process them; rather than quietly drop those passages from the corpus, we had them read instead by a smaller, open-weight version of the same underlying Qwen model that we ran ourselves on university computing resources, so that no passage went missing from the record because of a commercial filter’s judgment about its content.

A third reader works differently, and that difference is why we included it. Jev, a model built by the company TypeSafe, does not write an argument and then commit to an answer; asked each question separately, it returns a probability for every possible answer, so that where our two main readers give us ambiguity only indirectly, through the fact that they disagree, Jev gives a direct measure of how evenly a passage sits between two readings. We also tested, and excluded, a fourth reader, Meta’s Llama 3.3. Before admitting any model to the panel, we ran it on a fixed set of 277 calibration passages to check whether it was making distinctions at all; Llama placed more than ninety percent of its judgments in a single category and produced fluent, plausible arguments for every one of them. It is the failure a reader of the outputs alone would be least likely to catch, which is why we record it. The eight supplementary works, most of them still in copyright, were never sent to OpenAI or Alibaba: they were read by Jev and by the same open-weight Qwen model running on university hardware. On 754 public-domain passages that all three had read, that local model agreed with Qwen3-Max’s placement more often than GPT-5.4 did (84 against 73 percent of passages) and sat, on average, almost exactly where Qwen3-Max sits on the scale, which is what allows us to read the supplementary Faulkner novels alongside the rest of the corpus. 

Both GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max belong to a class of systems sometimes called “reasoning models,” designed to deliberate in text before answering a request. We did not want a bare number from them. For every judgment, we asked each reader to write out its case for the placement it chose, quoting the passage, along with its case against the strongest alternative, and we keep that written argument for every prediction. What we keep is the argument each reader was asked to produce, not the model’s internal deliberation, to which we have no access; but it is an argument made in the open, about specific words, and it can be checked against the passage. A system that returned only a bare number between 0 and 3 would tell us nothing about why it placed a passage there, and would give us no way to check whether that placement was a considered judgment or a coin flip that happened to land on a plausible-looking value.

A reader that gives the same reasoned answer nine times out of ten is doing something different from a reader that gives five different answers to the same passage across ten tries; the second case is closer to genuine uncertainty than to a stable judgment, and repeated, independent readings are what let us tell the two apart, the same distinction a critic would want to draw between a colleague who reliably argues for the same interpretation of a passage on repeated readings and one who is, in effect, guessing anew each time. Two examples make this concrete. The opening of *To the Lighthouse* renders James Ramsay’s joy at the prospect of a boat trip through an unmistakably interpretive, generalizing narrator: “since he belonged, even at the age of six, to that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that … James Ramsay, sitting on the floor cutting out pictures from the illustrated catalog of the Army and Navy Stores, endowed the picture of a refrigerator as his mother spoke with heavenly bliss.” Both readers, independently, placed this passage at level 0, psycho-narration—the most mediated point on the scale—and both grounded that placement in the same feature of the prose. GPT-5.4 wrote that “the passage clearly renders James Ramsay's consciousness, but it does so through a highly mediating narrator who explains and generalizes his mental process rather than presenting thought in his own idiom,” citing the phrase “that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that” as evidence that the sentence’s language belongs to the narrator’s analytic vocabulary, not the six-year-old’s. Qwen3-Max, reasoning independently, reached the same conclusion by citing the same kind of evidence: “these are analytic, classificatory statements about James’s psychology, not direct access to his thought process,” pointing to the same clause. Two systems asked the same question about the same sentence without seeing each other’s answers, and converged not only on a score but on which words in the passage justified it. Jev, our third reader, agrees on the level, though less emphatically, giving psycho-narration a probability of 0.60 against 0.39 for narrated monologue; and, tellingly, it does so while naming the technique indirect interior monologue. The scale and the older label, in other words, can come apart, and the boundary between psycho-narration and narrated monologue is where they most often do.

A passage from *The Waves* shows what happens further along the scale, and previews a distinction this chapter returns to throughout. Louis, hiding from the others, thinks: “I am on the other side of the hedge … O Lord, let them pass … I am green as a yew tree in the shade of the hedge. My hair is made of leaves. I am rooted to the middle of the earth. My body is a stalk.” Here, every reader—GPT-5.4, Qwen3-Max, and Jev, which gave the placement a probability of 0.73—placed the passage at level 3, autonomous monologue, agreeing that the unbroken first-person present tense, with no narratorial frame of any kind, put it at the least mediated extreme of the scale. But the readers did not agree on what to call it in Humphrey’s older vocabulary. Qwen3-Max named it a straightforward case of direct interior monologue on every reading, and Jev leaned the same way, though it gave nearly a third of its weight to imagery and hybrid. GPT-5.4 insisted, on every reading, on a “hybrid” label, arguing that the vegetal images—“My hair is made of leaves,” “My body is a stalk”—“do not merely decorate thought but enact the boy's hiding panic and self-effacing identification with the hedge,” so that the passage is not a total transcription of the character’s interiority. Here, the passage sits between two classifiable techniques, denying pure identification with one of them. This is a pattern that recurs throughout the corpus: readers converge on where a passage sits on Cohn’s continuous scale more often than they converge on what discrete name to give that position, which is one reason this chapter treats the scale, not the label, as the more trustworthy measurement. We should be careful not to overstate the point. For our two main readers, the level assigned is in practice almost entirely determined by the technique chosen: a passage named indirect interior monologue is placed at level 1 more than ninety-nine times in a hundred, so part of the scale’s greater agreement comes simply from its gathering several techniques under one position. Jev, asked about the two separately, places only about half of its “indirect” passages at level 1 and some four in ten at level 0. The scale’s advantage, then, is real but not independent of the typology, and Jev’s probabilities are our best evidence of where along the psycho-narration/narrated monologue boundary a passage actually sits. And it is the written arguments themselves, not just the numbers, that let us see this happening in specific, quotable language, rather than inferring it from statistics alone.

**What the Scale Measures, Rung by Rung**

Because everything that follows depends on it, it is worth setting out concretely
what separates one position on Cohn's scale from the next, and what a reader has
to see in the prose in order to move between them. The scale measures a single
thing: how much narratorial apparatus stands between us and a mind. At one end
the narrator holds the mind at arm's length and tells us about it; at the other
the apparatus disappears and we are left with the voice itself. The four
positions are the stages of that withdrawal, and because they are stages rather
than kinds, the interesting judgments are always about where one gives way to
the next.

At **level 0, psycho-narration**, a narrator reports on consciousness from
outside it, in a vocabulary that belongs to the narrator rather than the
character. The opening of *To the Lighthouse*, discussed above, is the type
case: a six-year-old's joy arrives through the proposition that he "belonged,
even at the age of six, to that great clan which cannot keep this feeling
separate from that." The sentence knows things about James Ramsay that James
Ramsay does not know about himself, and says them in words he could not have
supplied. Both readers placed it here, and both grounded the placement in that
same analytic clause.

At **level 1, narrated monologue**—free indirect discourse—the narrator's
sentence bends to accommodate the character's idiom without surrendering to it.
A passage from Richardson's *Pointed Roofs* shows the mechanism plainly. Miriam
works out that her employer's advertised English conversation is not happening:
"No doubt Fräulein Pfaff believed that they represented so many hours of English
conversation—and they did not. It was cheating, pure and simple." The
grammatical frame remains third person and past tense, and the narrator keeps a
light hand on the reins—"she discovered," "she soon realised"—but the verdict
*It was cheating, pure and simple* is Miriam's, in Miriam's register, not a
report of a verdict she reached. GPT-5.4 placed the passage at level 1 and said
so in those terms, citing the phrase as "her own inward verdict rather than
neutral narration"; Qwen3-Max independently agreed.

At **level 2, quoted monologue**, thought is given directly but remains framed:
the narration points at it and hands it over. In *Jacob's Room*, Mrs Durrant
appraises Jacob while winding wool: "'He is extraordinarily awkward,' she
thought, noticing how he fingered his socks. 'Yet so distinguished-looking.'"
The words are hers and unmediated—no narrator is paraphrasing her
estimate—but the quotation marks and the tag *she thought* keep them explicitly
bounded. Both readers named this quoted monologue and placed it at level 2.

At **level 3, autonomous monologue**, the frame is gone. Louis in *The Waves*:
"I am green as a yew tree in the shade of the hedge. My hair is made of leaves.
I am rooted to the middle of the earth." There is no tag, no containing
sentence, nothing to mark where narration stops and mind begins, because
narration has not been doing the holding.

How a reader actually arrives at one of these rather than its neighbour is the
part worth watching, and it is why we required an argument rather than a number.
Consider the two boundary cases. On the *Pointed Roofs* passage, GPT-5.4 named
psycho-narration as its strongest alternative and explained what pulled that
way—"did not...disturb her," "she discovered," "she soon realised," all of them,
as it put it, "psycho-narrative scaffolding"—before keeping level 1 on the
strength of "the moral phrasing *It was cheating, pure and simple*." On the
*Jacob's Room* passage it named the opposite alternative, autonomous monologue,
conceding that "'Yet so distinguished-looking.' has the clipped, immediate
quality of raw thought," and rejected it because "the framing tag 'she thought'
and the quotation marks keep the thought text explicitly bounded by narration."
In both cases the reader is not matching the passage against a definition but
adjudicating between two live readings on the evidence of particular words, and
recording which words decided it. That is the unit of judgment this chapter
works with: not a score, but a score with its warrant attached, and its strongest
rival named and dismissed in the open.

**Author Fingerprints Across the Corpus**

Cohn's scale is not, on its own, a computational instrument. Applying it to a single passage is an act of close reading; applying it consistently to twenty-five thousand passages across forty-four works requires a method that can hold a stable rubric across an enormous number of individual interpretive judgments without simply averaging away the disagreement that any two careful readers would have about some of them. The figures that follow are drawn from the full corpus: 25,157 passages and some 151,000 individual (passage, reader, run) judgments from our two main readers at three repeated runs. 

Before trusting a score of this kind, we needed to know whether it was tracking something real about the prose or simply reproducing whatever a language model’s prior notion of “stream of consciousness” already was. We ran two independent checks. The first tests mediation level against a small set of features computed without reading for meaning at all: the density of first-person pronouns, the ratio of past to non-past tense, and the density of internal punctuation (dashes, ellipses, semicolons), across all 16,953 passages that both main readers identified as rendering consciousness. If the scale were merely an artifact of model idiosyncrasy, there is no reason these counts should predict it. They do: passages with a higher proportion of first-person language sit reliably higher on the scale, closer to the character's own voice, and passages with a higher proportion of past-tense narration sit reliably lower, closer to the narrator's. Both relationships are moderate rather than strong, but they are consistent: they hold for each reader separately, Jev included, and they hold *within* each author's prose, not only between authors, which matters because Faulkner, say, is both more first-person and higher on the scale than James, and a pooled correlation could otherwise be carried by that contrast alone. Dashes and ellipses, the visual signature of a mind interrupting itself, mark less mediated prose as well, though more weakly; a pilot on ninety-seven passages had seemed to show the reverse, and at full scale that result did not survive. Semicolons are the more useful caution. Across the corpus as a whole, semicolon-heavy passages sit lower on the scale, but that is a difference between authors, chiefly the long subordinated periods of Henry James; within any single author's prose the relationship is negligible and runs, if anything, the other way. A feature that seems to predict mediation can turn out to be predicting the author, which is one more reason we never pool across authors without saying so.

The second check answers a sharper worry, one specific to canonical modernism: that a model asked how mediated a *Mrs Dalloway* or *Ulysses* passage is may be retrieving the critical consensus about that novel rather than reading the passage itself. LLMs are demonstrably capable of reproducing well-known public-domain texts closely enough to raise exactly this concern, and *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *The Waves*, *Ulysses*, and Faulkner's two most famous novels are among the most-discussed works in the corpus. We tested this directly by masking character names and other title-identifying features in a stratified sample of 177 passages from six of these titles and rescoring the masked versions in a blind fashion; 166 of them remained scorable, the other eleven losing their status as renderings of consciousness once the names were gone, which is itself a small sign of how much a few proper nouns can carry. If the model were substantially retrieving the book's reputation rather than reading it, masking should produce large, inconsistent shifts. It does not: masked scores correlate with original scores at 0.902 across the sample, with a mean shift of \-0.042 on the 0–3 scale and a mean *absolute* shift of only 0.253—noise, not a systematic collapse. The correlation is not uniform across titles (it is highest for *To the Lighthouse*, at 0.95, and lowest for *Mrs Dalloway*, at 0.74, itself a small, specific finding worth further attention rather than an average to be trusted blindly), but nowhere does it suggest the scale is mostly reading the byline. Because the sample was drawn evenly across the four levels, it speaks to individual passages, not to how whole novels rank against one another.

Taken together, these two checks do not prove that a mediation score is equivalent to a trained narratologist's judgment; two LLMs sharing similar training data and similar failure modes is not the same thing as two independent human coders, and we do not claim otherwise, though the presence of a third, differently built reader, for whom the same grammatical relationships hold, makes the point less acute than it would otherwise be. But they do show that the number answers to the passage's own grammar and content, not to a model’s untethered impression of what a famous novel ought to sound like.

With the scale validated, the first question is the simplest: does the mediation level, averaged across an author's entire output, differ from author to author in a way that aligns with anything criticism would recognize? It does. Here and below we give figures pooled across our two main readers, as the most compact summary, and say where the two diverge enough to matter; they agree on how authors and novels rank far more than on where the scale’s zero point sits. Faulkner's three public-domain novels average 1.67 on the 0–3 scale, with a spread (standard deviation) of 1.26 and forty percent of his scored passages landing at the least mediated extreme, autonomous monologue. That average conceals a split. *The Sound and the Fury* and *As I Lay Dying* have the profile of a writer whose SOC prose oscillates between very little mediation and quite a lot of it, rarely settling in the middle; his first novel, *Soldiers' Pay* (1926), averages just under 1. Henry James sits at the opposite pole: across six novels spanning nearly thirty years, a mean of 0.41, a spread of only 0.61, and two-thirds of his passages at the most mediated level, psycho-narration, a narrator who reports on consciousness from outside and rarely relinquishes that position, which is exactly the role James's fiction is usually assigned in accounts of the novel's prehistory to stream of consciousness proper. May Sinclair sits near him, at 0.55, with nearly sixty percent of her passages at psycho-narration, and Ford Madox Ford, across the whole of *Parade's End* as well as *The Good Soldier*, at 0.80, divided mostly between psycho-narration and narrated monologue. Joyce, the writer against whom Woolf's supposed quietness is conventionally measured, averages 1.37, roughly a third of his passages at each of level 0, level 1, and level 3, a genuinely bimodal-leaning profile rather than a settled one. Dorothy Richardson, whose *Pilgrimage* volumes are usually grouped with Woolf as the other major "technique" writer of the group, averages 0.91 across nine volumes, with a comparatively tight spread of 0.77, sixty-one percent of her passages clustering specifically at narrated monologue, the most concentrated at that level of any author in the corpus.

And Woolf: a mean of 0.82, a spread of 0.88, forty-three percent of her passages at psycho-narration and forty percent at narrated monologue, with only seventeen percent reaching quoted or autonomous monologue at all. Our two readers differ noticeably over Woolf, as they do over every author in the corpus: GPT-5.4 places just over half of her passages at psycho-narration and a quarter at narrated monologue, Qwen3-Max fewer than a third at psycho-narration and more than half at narrated monologue. Under either reader, though, she sits below Richardson, Joyce, and Faulkner and above James and Sinclair; only her position relative to Ford depends on who is reading. On the numbers alone, this places her prose alongside Ford's and just short of Richardson's, well away from Faulkner's or, on this measure, even Joyce's, as far as it goes, evidence for exactly the "quiet" reputation the field has assigned her. Joyce's own developmental arc across the corpus's three Joyce titles confirms a version of the standard account on its own terms: *Dubliners* (1914) averages 0.38, *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* (1916) averages 0.69, and *Ulysses* (1922) averages 1.77, a monotonic march toward less mediation, under each reader separately, that matches the way literary history usually tells Joyce's story, one novel's technique building on the last.

But an author-level mean, however well validated, describes only the center of a distribution, and the word "quiet" has always implied not just where Woolf's prose sits on average but that it sits there consistently, novel after novel, in a way that would make her body of work legible as a single style. That claim is testable independently of the mean, and it is where the numbers stop confirming the standard account.

**Woolf’s SOC Variation, Novel to Novel**

The reception history this chapter has been describing treats Woolf's SOC as, in effect, a fixed style: quieter than Joyce's, more grammatically composed, essentially the same technique deployed across a career. Cohn's scale lets us test that assumption directly, not by asking how Woolf compares to other authors on average, but by asking how much a given Woolf novel's mean mediation score differs from the mean of her other novels, whether “Woolf's SOC” is one thing measured ten times, or ten things that happen to share a name.

It is closer to the second. Using an intraclass correlation (ICC), a variance-components measure of between-title consistency that corrects for the fact that an author with more titles in a sample is otherwise structurally advantaged relative to an author with fewer, Woolf's ten novels score 0.561, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of \[0.533, 0.588\] (0.498 and 0.544 under GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max separately): more than half of the total variation in mediation level across her corpus falls between books rather than within them. No other author in the comparative set matches this, though three come nearer than a smaller corpus first suggested. Joyce scores 0.358 \[0.323, 0.394\]. Faulkner, now on three titles, scores 0.219 \[0.173, 0.269\], rising to 0.440 under Qwen3-Max alone, but the figure rests entirely on the distance between *Soldiers' Pay* and the two novels that followed it: without *Soldiers' Pay* it falls to 0.037. Sinclair scores 0.185, much of it the move into *Mary Olivier* (without it, 0.104). The writers with genuinely independent books and a settled manner look settled: Ford, across five novels, scores 0.025, and Henry James, across six, 0.012. Richardson's nine volumes score 0.029, but this needs an immediate caveat rather than a footnote: *Pilgrimage*'s volumes are installments of one continuous narrative rather than independent novels, so low between-volume variance is close to a definitional fact about serial fiction, not a finding about a fixed authorial style, and we do not use her as a comparison case on this axis for that reason. (Only *Oberland*, at 0.58, strays outside a narrow band.) The three late volumes, which only Jev has read in full, do not change the picture, and we can say that with more confidence than the arithmetic alone would warrant. Those volumes reach us in a different typographic convention from the first nine: they mark dialogue with single quotation marks where the earlier volumes use double, a difference of printing rather than of prose. That distinction falls precisely on the feature Cohn's second level depends on, since quoted monologue is defined by thought being *framed*, and a reader that took its cue from the glyphs rather than the grammar could register a change of typesetter as a change of style. We tested it rather than assuming it away: we converted all 865 passages of the late volumes from single to double quotation marks and rescored them. Jev's reading barely moved—quoted monologue shifted by half a percentage point, the mean by two hundredths of a level—which is to say it reads framing from the sentence rather than from the punctuation. The open-weight Qwen reader, run over the same volumes, behaves differently and more cautiously: under that model the share of passages called quoted monologue is markedly lower in the single-quote volumes than in the double-quote ones, a gap large enough that we would not want to read its placements of late *Pilgrimage* as directly comparable to its placements of the early volumes. Sensitivity to typography, in other words, is a property of particular readers rather than of the scale itself, and it is detectable here only because the corpus happens to contain one author's continuous work in two printing conventions—an accident we would not have thought to engineer, and would not have caught without a second reader to disagree with the first.

It would be easy to assume this entire result is *The Waves* doing all the work, and a large part of it is: leave-one-title-out sensitivity shows that removing *The Waves* alone drops Woolf's ICC from 0.561 to 0.199. That is a real and important qualification. It is not, however, the whole story, for two reasons. First, the fair comparison is not Woolf without *The Waves* against everyone else's full figures, but each author with their own single most extreme title removed, and on that footing Woolf's 0.199 stands above Joyce without *Ulysses* (0.117), Sinclair without *Mary Olivier* (0.104), and Faulkner without *Soldiers' Pay* (0.037). The ordering holds under each reader separately, though under Qwen3-Max Sinclair comes close: Woolf minus her most unusual novel is still more variable, book to book, than any of them minus theirs. Second, removing any of Woolf's other nine titles individually moves the ICC only slightly, from 0.563 (without *Flush*) to 0.612 (without *The Years*), meaning that whatever is driving Woolf's between-book variance beyond *The Waves* is distributed across her career rather than concentrated in some second outlier. Nor is the finding an artifact of including *Flush* and *Orlando*, Woolf's two genre-heterogeneous mock-biographies, in a sample otherwise composed of more conventional novels; excluding both actually raises Woolf's ICC to 0.586. However the calculation is sliced, two claims survive: *The Waves* is genuinely, measurably discontinuous with the rest of Woolf's fiction, a departure at least as large as any single title's departure from its author's baseline in this corpus (only *Soldiers' Pay*, under one of our readers, matches it); and the other nine novels, considered on their own, still vary by book more than a "settled style" account predicts.

A mean, though, discards exactly the thing narratologists are usually most interested in about free indirect discourse: not a passage's average distance from the narrator, but its *movement*, the sentence-by-sentence oscillation between voices that gives the technique its characteristic texture. A mean of 0.89 for *Mrs Dalloway* is compatible with prose that alternates violently between levels 0 and 2, or with prose that sits flatly at level 1 throughout, and criticism has generally assumed the former. The full within-title distributions, and a measure of how strongly one passage's level predicts the next passage's level (a lag-1 autocorrelation along the text), let us ask directly which picture is closer to true, novel by novel.

The answer varies enough to complicate the mean-level story on its own terms. *Mrs Dalloway* (mean 0.89) is in fact the most internally concentrated of Woolf's mid-range novels: seventy-four percent of its passages land at narrated monologue specifically, with only seven percent reaching level 2 and one percent level 3, a narrow, mid-scale band rather than an oscillation between extremes, and one both readers see. *To the Lighthouse* (mean 0.76) looks similar: sixty-seven percent at narrated monologue, only four percent above it. *The Years* (mean 0.98), despite an almost identical mean to *Mrs Dalloway*, has a different shape: it reaches quoted monologue, level 2, far more often than *Dalloway* does, though the margin depends on the reader—a third of its passages for GPT-5.4 and an eighth for Qwen3-Max, against a tenth and a fiftieth for *Mrs Dalloway*—so that the two novels' averages suggest a nearer kinship in technique than their texture bears out. *Jacob's Room* (mean 0.59) is a case where the method's caution matters. Pooled, its passages seem to split almost evenly between levels 0 and 1, but that split belongs to our readers rather than to the book: GPT-5.4 places most of the novel at psycho-narration, Qwen3-Max most of it at narrated monologue, and each, on its own, sees a book concentrated at one level, disagreeing only about which. *Jacob's Room* sits, that is, squarely on the seam between the two, the place where the narrator's report and the character's thought are hardest to tell apart. *The Waves* alone, as expected, sits overwhelmingly at the far end: sixty-seven percent of its passages at autonomous monologue, only four percent at psycho-narration, the closest thing in the entire Woolf corpus to Faulkner's profile rather than James's.

Autocorrelation adds a further, and in some ways more surprising, axis of variation, one no account of Woolf's "quiet" style has had the resolution to notice before. *Between the Acts* (0.366), *Orlando* (0.325), and *To the Lighthouse* (0.315) show the strongest tendency for a passage's mediation level to predict the next passage's level, registers that, once entered, tend to be sustained for a stretch of text, comparable in strength to Faulkner's *As I Lay Dying* (0.327) and above most of Richardson's nine *Pilgrimage* volumes (which range from 0.08 to 0.36). *Flush* (0.050) and *Jacob's Room* (0.051), by contrast, sit near the floor of the entire corpus, alongside Henry James's *The Wings of the Dove* (0.03) and *What Maisie Knew* (0.07) and Sinclair's *Audrey Craven* (0.05): register that moves passage to passage almost independently of what came before. *Mrs Dalloway* (0.177) and *The Years* (0.164) land in between, closer to the low end. What this shows is that "how consistently a Woolf novel holds its register" and "how mediated a Woolf novel is on average" are not the same variable and do not move together: *Between the Acts* is both comparatively unmediated, among the least mediated of her novels after *The Waves*, and comparatively sustained in that register, holding it for a stretch of prose at a time; *Jacob's Room* is comparatively mediated on average and almost entirely unpredictable passage to passage, its narrator's distance from Jacob's mind resetting with each new paragraph rather than settling into a discernible pattern. Two novels can share a mean and differ completely in texture, or share a texture and differ in mean; Woolf's ten novels do both, in different combinations, novel by novel.

Two further observations belong here, both of which run counter to a career-arc reading of these results. *The Waves* (1931) sits chronologically between *Orlando* (1928) and *Flush* (1933), two of the most heavily mediated novels in Woolf's output; the departure is not the culmination of a steady drift toward less mediation across her career, but a single, bounded event, bracketed on both sides by a return to something closer to her earlier register. That shape, we should add, is not Woolf's alone. Read across all eight of his novels from *Soldiers' Pay* to *Absalom, Absalom!*, a reading only Jev has so far given in full, Faulkner's career has the same profile: *The Sound and the Fury* and *As I Lay Dying* stand well clear of the more mediated novels on either side of them. What distinguishes Woolf is not that she made one such excursion, but how far *The Waves* travels and how much her other nine novels vary among themselves. And across the full forty-four-title corpus, a title-level test for a diachronic trend toward less classifiable, more taxonomy-resistant prose over the 1875–1941 span found no reliable trend at all (Spearman's rho \= 0.02, p \= 0.91). One of our readers, GPT-5.4, does flag passages as resisting every named technique more often in later novels; but our other main reader almost never makes that judgment at all, and a trend visible to one reader only is not one we can attribute to the novels. Whatever is happening across Woolf's career, it is not part of a broader modernist drift that a mere publication date predicts. If Woolf's fiction has a shape, it is not a line moving toward one destination; it is closer to a set of discrete, revisited choices, of which *The Waves* is the one time she declined the choice she otherwise kept making.

If that shape is not a line, it is worth saying what it is instead, because the
alternative has a name the period's criticism has reserved for other writers.
Set Woolf's sequence beside Joyce's. His three novels, given above, climb
without once reversing: each sits further from the narrator than the one before.
That is development in the ordinary sense, a technique found, extended, and
pushed to its limit, and it is the shape literary history has always given him.
Woolf's ten run 0.39, 0.37, 0.59, 0.89, 0.76, 0.51, 2.50, 0.37, 0.98, 1.02.
Five of her nine transitions move toward less mediation and four move back.
*Flush*, in 1933, sits at 0.37, which is where *Night and Day* sat in 1919, six
novels and eighteen years earlier, and within a hundredth of where she had begun
in *The Voyage Out*. A writer extending a technique could not have written
*Flush* after *The Waves*. A writer for whom the degree of mediation is itself
the variable under test could, and did.

This is where the quiet reading mistook its unit rather than its measurement.
The experimentation the period is known for is sentence-level: Joyce's syntax,
the grammatical disfigurements, prose that advertises its difficulty on the page.
Measured that way Woolf is the quieter writer, and the field's verdict follows
reasonably from its premise. But the scale locates her experiment one level up.
What varies across her career is not the sentence but each book's whole
settlement with the minds it renders: how close the narration will stand, how
much apparatus it will keep, whether consciousness will be reported, inflected,
quoted, or simply let run. Every novel re-opens that question, answers it
differently, and the answers do not accumulate.

Both things are true at once, and the distinction is what the measurement buys:
Woolf's prose is less grammatically disruptive than Joyce's *and* her career is
more internally various than his. The second claim stayed invisible for as long
as experimentation was assumed to be something a sentence does. It becomes
visible the moment one asks of each book in turn how far it stands from the mind
it renders, which is the question Cohn's scale was built to ask, and the question
a corpus is needed to ask ten times over.

*Close Reading at Scale: "Time Passes"*

The corpus-wide results speak to Woolf's SOC across whole novels; it is worth also asking what the same method can do at the scale of a single, already-famous crux. "Time Passes," the short middle section of *To the Lighthouse* in which the Ramsays' house stands empty for ten years and Mrs Ramsay's death is disposed of in a single bracketed aside, is a canonical site of exactly this kind of narratorial strangeness in Woolf criticism, a section critics have long treated as doing something formally unusual, without always agreeing on what. It is also, in our data, the single site across *To the Lighthouse* and *Mrs Dalloway* where a reader's antimimetic flag actually fires. That flag is reserved for a passage that, for an explicit textual reason, fits none of Humphrey's named techniques at all, not merely one the reader is unsure about. (Across the full corpus it is rare but not confined to this section: it appears most often in *Ulysses*, and occasionally in *Orlando* and *The Waves*.) That made it worth a properly powered second look: rescoring all thirty-three passages of the section at ten runs per reader instead of three, rather than resting an interpretive claim on a single low-probability hit.

"Time Passes" is famous for its strangeness, and the natural assumption going in was that strangeness would register as *less* mediated prose: thought breaking further free of narratorial control, the mind rendered rawer and closer to its own voice, as in Faulkner's or Joyce's more radical passages. It turns out to be the opposite. Across the section, mediation scores skew markedly toward the heavily mediated end of the scale compared to the novel's baseline, and the great majority of its stable, SOC-positive passages land at psycho-narration, the *most* mediated point on Cohn's scale, not the least. Whatever is strange about "Time Passes," then, is not that Woolf's narrator recedes; it is that the narrator's ordinary move, interpreting a mind from outside, "she felt," "it seemed as if," keeps firing on content that increasingly has no mind, in any ordinary sense, to interpret.

That displacement sits in almost no space at all: two sentences, out of thirty-three passages, in which mental predicates are given not to a character but to something that is not a person. In one, "certain airs, detached from the body of the wind," are described entering the house "questioning and wondering," asking, "Were they allies? Were they enemies?" In the other, the room's own silence is personified the same way, "weaving" the cries of birds and the drone of the fields into itself and "folding them round the house in silence," the same rendering of thought-like agency in something that has no mind to think it. On these two sentences, and on almost no others in the whole sample, our two main readers, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max, come apart in a way that is not noise. Across ten repeated readings of the first sentence, GPT-5.4 does not scatter across many answers; it lands, almost evenly, on two distinct positions, each independently reproduced: five readings that place the passage among Humphrey's techniques, under strain, as an unusually mediated simulation of a state of mind, and five that refuse it every named technique, arguing that thought-like inquiry without a "plausible single consciousness to own it" resists "the taxonomy's assumption that rendered consciousness belongs to a character mind." Notably, in all ten readings it keeps the passage at psycho-narration on Cohn's scale; what it doubts is the typology, not the scale. Qwen3-Max, reading the identical sentence, registers almost no strain at all: all ten readings place it at psycho-narration, nine of them without any strain on its chosen technique, the personified airs treated as ordinary narratorial imagery rather than as anything the typology cannot hold. That calm is Qwen3-Max's habit generally, however. Across the whole corpus it almost never judges that a passage fits no named technique, so its reading here reflects a disposition it brings to every sentence as much as a judgment about this one.

This is, in miniature, close to the theoretical debate Monika Fludernik and, following her, Jan Alber and Brian Richardson have had about "unnatural" narrative technique more broadly: whether devices that seem to exceed realist consciousness-representation are a genuinely distinct category, or whether they are simply conventions not yet naturalized by a given reader's expectations. Our data does not answer that debate, but it does show it actually happening in a specific, quotable pair of sentences, between two readers with different priors about how far a figure of speech can carry an interior state before it stops being a figure and starts being a problem for the categories used to describe it. It also lets us say something more precise than "Woolf's SOC sometimes exceeds measurement": across *To the Lighthouse* and *Mrs Dalloway*, this device, thought-like predicates attached to a non-human antecedent, is the one place, nearly the only place, where whether Humphrey's named techniques can hold a passage depends on which reader is doing the holding. It is worth noticing what holds there for both readers: Cohn's scale. Whatever is thinking in these sentences, both readers agree that it is being rendered from outside.

**Conclusion: Restraint as a Repeated Choice**

This data does not yield a corrected measure of how quiet Woolf's SOC really is relative to Joyce's, nor a definitive answer to whether she belongs among literature's more or less radical stream-of-consciousness writers, and it would be a mistake to read it that way. The comparative frame this chapter opened by describing, Woolf measured against a chaos she declines, a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, turns out to survive at the level of the author-wide mean: on average, across her whole output, Woolf's prose really is more mediated than Joyce's or Faulkner's, more concentrated at psycho-narration and narrated monologue, closer to Richardson's profile than to theirs. What does not survive is the assumption bundled inside that comparison, that the mean describes a *style*, something Woolf does consistently, novel after novel, the way "chaos" is assumed to be something Joyce does consistently. Nine of her ten novels occupy a band on Cohn's scale that is far wider and far more internally varied, in both level and texture, than a single adjective like "quiet" can hold; the tenth, *The Waves*, leaves that band entirely, as unmediated by our measure as the most radical of Faulkner's novels, and does so as a single bounded event in the middle of her career rather than its culmination, a shape Faulkner's own career shares, though few novels in the corpus depart so far from their author's baseline. The right description of Woolf's stream of consciousness, on this evidence, is not a fixed point on a scale running from control to chaos, but a record of a writer choosing restraint, repeatedly, in different ways in different books, and once, deliberately, choosing not to.

This is also what a computational method can contribute to a problem that close reading alone has spent a century worrying at without resolving. It was never going to settle whether SOC is a genre or a technique, or produce a scale finer than Cohn's that finally holds without remainder. Our two principal readers, trained on similar data and prone to similar failure modes, are not independent coders in the sense inter-rater reliability normally requires, even with a third, differently built reader beside them; and a computational finding, however well validated against surface features and blind to a novel's title, still answers a question a critic posed to it rather than discovering the question on its own. But the method can apply an existing, theoretically grounded scale with total consistency across an entire body of work, at a resolution no reader could sustain unaided across forty-four works and twenty-five thousand passages; it can locate, with precision, where that consistency is real and where it is a mean concealing real variation underneath; and it can follow the one place where the older typology breaks down while the scale holds, all the way to two sentences and a disagreement each reader can actually articulate. This chapter's contribution is a way of seeing, at the scale of a career rather than a passage, that what Woolf's readers have been calling one style was several, and that the moment she left all of them behind can be dated, measured, and read closely, on the same page.
