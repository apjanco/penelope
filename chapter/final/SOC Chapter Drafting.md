Was Woolf Ever “Quiet”? A Computational Evaluation of Levels of Mediation in Stream of Consciousness Novels

Virginia Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of stream-of-consciousness (SOC) narration, the ever-familiar term in modernist studies—and modern fiction generally—for literary works which foreground the idiosyncratic movements of the mind as it attempts to render the intricacies of experience. Yet scholars are sometimes hard-pressed to identify the ways Woolf’s particular strain of SOC breaks from tradition, finding ambiguity and blurriness in her use of accepted SOC technique, as well as surprising adherence to older modes of narration where more radical expression might be expected. When it comes to SOC form, Woolf’s “innovations … have not been clearly recognized” because, perhaps, her fiction contains less obvious markers of innovative narrative technique: “while \[SOC\] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom’s soliloquy at the end of *Ulysses*, Woolf’s innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly” (Zhang 133). We argue that this gap exposes a need for a different formal criteria for recognizing Woolf’s unique narrative experimentation, one which considers SOC on a *scale of narrative mediation* rather than as a binaristic set of techniques such as those listed by Zhang and other SOC scholars. Through a computational, large language model (LLM) methodology drawn from Dorrit Cohn’s concept of levels of mediation, we suggest that Woolf’s ambiguous position in the history and development of SOC comes from a continual, yet constantly shifting, book-by-book experimentation with narrative modes. 

Arriving at a clearer sense of Woolf’s particular mode of SOC involves stripping back some of the assumptions about her writing that have persisted since early modernist scholarship. Woolf is generally thought to have a “more quiet” expression of SOC than some of her contemporaries, particularly James Joyce, who often defines the more radical strain of modernist narrative experimentation. Many scholars describe Woolf’s SOC through a comparison with Joyce. Anne Fernihough argues that “Joyce's technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf's, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the *chaos* of the inner life” (66, emphasis in original), and David Dowling, drawing from David Daiches, notes Woolf's relative “control” over even her most wandering passages: “Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a ‘tidy mind’ compared with that of Joyce” (48). Overall, one gets the impression that Woolf’s narration surprisingly does *not* include the standard techniques of other modernist writing. For instance, Franco Moretti describes Woolf’s narratorial control in *To the Lighthouse* through negations: 

If, at certain moments, Lily Briscoe is seized by panic and on the point of losing control, Woolf's syntax is able to check any such drift, and give form to chaos … Nothing ungrammatical, here; no leveling parataxis, or random associations. In sentences woven into a lucid sequence leading towards their goal … narrative consciousness has regained the upper hand. (172)

These critics evaluate Woolf’s “controlled” syntax against a particular experimental standard—“chaos”—rather than constructing a definition of SOC out of Woolf’s own instantiation of it. When scholars do pay closer attention to Woolf’s SOC technique specifically, it is closely associated with free indirect discourse, or similarly what Dorrit Cohn calls “narrated monologue,” a “character’s mental discourse in the guise of the narrator’s discourse” (14). This technique, Cohn writes, “needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction” (115). In other words, Woolf's primary choice of SOC technique was less a clean break from tradition and more a subtle shift, situated oppositely from the “rival technique of the unsignaled ‘Joycean’ monologue” (112). Dora Zhang convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionized free indirect discourse more than others have acknowledged, but still this perpetuates Woolf’s association with a single named technique.

Taken together, these assumptions describe a Woolf who is legible to SOC criticism only by comparison, and only ever in the negative: measured against a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, a chaos she declines to produce, and a free indirect discourse so familiar it no longer seems to require close technical scrutiny. This comparative frame, we suggest, has obscured a more basic question that the field has not yet put to Woolf's fiction directly: not how her stream of consciousness differs from Joyce's, but where, exactly, and how consistently, her prose sits on the single continuous scale that both writers' techniques share, novel by novel and even passage by passage. Answering that question requires more than another close reading of a single passage, however skillful; it requires applying that scale across an entire body of work, consistently, at a resolution fine enough to see whether “quiet” describes one style or several.

To approach such a question, our chapter makes two arguments that build on each other. First, we turn to Cohn’s mediation scale as something that can be applied consistently across an entire body of work, not just invoked in a single close reading, and ask what that consistency reveals about how modernist SOC prose actually distributes itself along the scale, novel by novel and author by author. Second, we evaluate Woolf’s specific relation to this larger corpus, asking whether her fiction has a single position on that scale at all, or whether her reputation for a settled, recognizable “quiet” technique is itself a critical simplification that a passage-by-passage measurement can correct. **\[give an actual preview into the conclusions we come to\].** These two arguments rely on a particular kind of computational method: large language models used not as classifiers producing a single confident verdict, but as resampled, independently reasoning readers whose agreement and disagreement are themselves data, analyzed with the same statistical caution a psychometrician would bring to any panel of raters.

Finally, this speaks to a larger paradox about SOC as a literary phenomenon: that despite its ubiquity it has been notoriously difficult to characterize concretely. Hoyt Long and Richard Jean So sum up the problem succinctly in their large-scale computational study of SOC narration across fictional traditions: “no one can agree on what \[SOC\] is, yet everyone seems to know it when they see it” (346). For such a widely-discussed literary phenomenon, it is striking that SOC continues to be undecidable in its particularities, and also that it endures as something which wants to be particularized. Perhaps, however, the persistent scholarly disagreement over what SOC is and how it works is not a defect to be engineered away, but the most useful signal a computational method can capture about the form. **Reading Stream of Consciousness along Dorrit Cohn’s Scale of Mediation**

In order to apply a systematic computational method to the question of what defines Woolf’s particular mode of SOC, we had to decide how to differentiate modes of SOC in the first place. But this itself is still a major area of debate. Originating from William James's description of human thought and brought into literary studies by May Sinclair in a review of Dorothy Richardson’s novels, the metaphor of the "stream" of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. As a broad literary genre, SOC has come to describe works that “attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual” by showing “not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced" (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SOC’s particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. Many early attempts to resolve this issue have reached for a typology: a set of named categorical techniques—interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and so on—into which a passage of prose can be sorted. Robert Humphrey’s 1954 *Stream of Consciousness in the Modern Novel* is the foundational instance and remains one of the field’s most cited points of reference. While Humphrey acknowledges from the outset the tricky ambiguity of SOC as both genre and technique, he ultimately uses the book to attempt to delineate buckets of techniques into which passages can be sorted. 

Indeed, a typology built from named, discrete categories has a specific cost: it asks a passage to declare itself as one thing among several, when what critics (including Humphrey himself) have actually described is a matter of degree. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in their introduction to a special issue on SOC, “a definitive definition of the elusive label ‘stream of consciousness’ is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized” (2). They go on to take issue with the critical impulse historically to “classify” SOC based on these supposedly measurable categories: “all stream of consciousness writing is formally specific only to itself, but there has been a tendency to classify all such writing as adhering to the same technique or single form” (3). This “tendency” in part has to do with the idea that SOC in its purest form was a complete shift in how consciousness was portrayed in the novel: that true SOC completely altered standard modes of narration, through new syntactic structures that “may or may not respect conventional grammatical, logical, or rhetorical paradigms … leveling or at least reconfiguring the traditional hierarchies ordering the parts of a sentence” (Mieszkowski 169-70). Thinking of SOC as simply experimental technique forecloses the possibility of recognizing the myriad ways different authors deploy both traditional and new narrative modes toward the project 

We needed a way to appropriately measure SOC in all its fluidity—to pin down a concrete and precise formal way of measuring it while also not imposing historical assumptions about the form onto Woolf’s texts. In her seminal narratological study of interiority in fiction, *Transparent Minds* (1978), Dorrit Cohn pushes back against the prevailing view that SOC was as “radical a break with tradition as has been assumed,” instead placing it within a broader discussion of how authors have chosen to mediate the distance between narrator and character (9). Rather than asking which of several techniques a SOC passage contains, Cohn asks a single, continuous question: how much mediation stands between the narrator’s voice and the character’s consciousness? Passages narrating character interiority are evaluated along a mediation scale running from “psycho-narration” (the narrator’s own account of a character’s inner life, from outside), though narrated monologue (the character’s thought rendered in the narrator’s own grammar, without quotation), to quoted monologue (an interior utterance set off, however lightly, as the character’s own words), and finally to autonomous monologue (thought presented with no narratorial frame at all). Cohn’s scale offers a corrective to the classificatory impulse of SOC scholarship while also still providing a systematic framework that can be scaled up computationally.

*Measuring Mediation at Scale: Methodology and Verification*

This chapter takes Cohn’s scale as its starting point and principal computational instrument, and asks what a large corpus of SOC prose looks like when read along it, resampled, at scale, by multiple independent readers, human and machine. Our corpus centers on Virginia Woolf's ten novels, *The Voyage Out*, *Night and Day*, *Jacob's Room*, *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *Orlando*, *The Waves*, *Flush*, *The Years*, and *Between the Acts*, alongside a comparative set of \[number of\] novels spanning James Joyce, Dorothy Richardson, William Faulkner, Henry James, May Sinclair, and Ford Madox Ford. It is worth noting that preliminary versions of our research began with Humphrey’s typology instead of Cohn’s scale. We found that while Humphrey’s categories do describe particular techniques found repeatedly throughout SOC writing, they were simultaneously too broad and too binary to accurately be applied to full texts. Multiple rounds of testing produced highly ambiguous results: models were frequently unsure about what passage belonged to what category. A catch-all “hybrid” category was offered as a solution to passages which combined multiple techniques, but this made accurately measuring categorical distribution impossible. 

For every passage in the corpus, we gave each of \[three?\] LLMs the passage itself, quoted exactly as it appears in the novel, along with a plain-language description of Cohn's four levels, not just “0 through 3,” but a description of what each position on the scale means, illustrated with an example. We then asked the reader to do four things: decide whether the passage renders consciousness at all, as opposed to ordinary external narration or dialogue; if it does, place it on the scale; name which of Humphrey's older, more familiar technique-names it most resembles, as a secondary point of reference rather than the primary judgment; and, crucially, justify that placement using specific wording quoted from the passage itself, not a general impression of its style. We also required each reader to name the next-most-plausible alternative placement and explain why it rejected that alternative in favor of the one it chose and modeled on the way a seminar asks a reader to address the strongest available counter-reading, not simply assert their own. A judgment that cannot survive stating the other side, in our view, is not yet a finding. Because any single reading, human or machine, might be a fluke, each passage was scored several times. Each LLM read and scored the same passage three times across the corpus as a whole, and ten times for a small number of passages which we examine closely in a later section of this chapter. 

We should note exactly what “reading” means here in practice. Two LLMs did almost all of the reading this chapter draws on: GPT-5.4, OpenAI’s most capable general-purpose model at the time of this study, and Qwen3-Max, the equivalent flagship system built by Alibaba. We chose two systems from two different companies, built on different underlying training data and different design philosophies, on purpose: if both, independently, tend to agree about where a passage sits, that agreement is harder to dismiss as one model’s bias than if we had simply asked the same system the same question twice. A handful of passages, eleven in total, scattered across *Ulysses*, *The Sound and the Fury*, and *The Ambassadors*, were never read by Qwen3-Max at all, because Alibaba’s own automatic content filters refused to process them; rather than quietly drop those passages from the corpus, we had them read instead by a third, smaller system, a open-weight version of the same underlying Qwen model that we ran ourselves on university computing resources, so that no passage went missing from the record because of a commercial filter’s judgment about its content. A further, smaller round of scoring, described below where it becomes relevant, added two more independently run systems, built by Meta and the French company Mistral, to a subset of the corpus, as a check on whether the picture changes again with yet another pair of unrelated readers.

Both GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max belong to a class of systems sometimes called “reasoning models,” designed to generate “thinking” text in response to a request before answering it. Given a passage, the system does not simply output a number; it first produces a paragraph or more of visible deliberation, considering the passage's grammar, weighing more than one possible reading, and only then committing to a placement on Cohn’s scale. This is not a hidden process we have to infer from the outside. For every prediction, we record the text of the reasoning trace. A system that returned only a bare number between 0 and 3 would tell us nothing about why it placed a passage there, and would give us no way to check whether that placement was a considered judgment or a coin flip that happened to land on a plausible-looking value.

A reader that gives the same reasoned answer nine times out of ten is doing something different from a reader that gives five different answers to the same passage across ten tries; the second case is closer to genuine uncertainty than to a stable judgment, and repeated, independent readings are what let us tell the two apart, the same distinction a critic would want to draw between a colleague who reliably argues for the same interpretation of a passage on repeated readings and one who is, in effect, guessing anew each time. Two examples make this concrete. The opening of *To the Lighthouse* renders James Ramsay’s joy at the prospect of a boat trip through an unmistakably interpretive, generalizing narrator: “since he belonged, even at the age of six, to that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that … James Ramsay, sitting on the floor cutting out pictures from the illustrated catalog of the Army and Navy Stores, endowed the picture of a refrigerator as his mother spoke with heavenly bliss.” Both readers, independently, placed this passage at level 0, psycho-narration—the most mediated point on the scale—and both grounded that placement in the same feature of the prose. GPT-5.4 wrote that “the passage clearly renders James’s consciousness, but it does so through an authoritative narrator who explains and generalizes his mental process rather than presenting thought in his own idiom,” citing the phrase “that great clan which cannot keep this feeling separate from that” as evidence that the sentence’s language belongs to the narrator’s analytic vocabulary, not the six-year-old’s. Qwen3-Max, reasoning independently, reached the same conclusion by citing the same kind of evidence: “these are analytic, classificatory statements about James’s psychology, not direct access to his thought process,” pointing to the same clause. Two systems asked the same question about the same sentence without seeing each other’s answers, and converged not only on a score but on which words in the passage justified it.

A passage from *The Waves* shows what happens further along the scale, and previews a distinction this chapter returns to throughout. Louis, hiding from the others, thinks: “I am on the other side of the hedge … O Lord, let them pass … I am green as a yew tree in the shade of the hedge. My hair is made of leaves. I am rooted to the middle of the earth. My body is a stalk.” Here, every reader—GPT-5.4, Qwen3-Max, and, in the later round, the Meta-built system as well—placed the passage at level 3, autonomous monologue, agreeing that the unbroken first-person present tense, with no narratorial frame of any kind, put it at the least mediated extreme of the scale. But the readers did not agree on what to call it in Humphrey’s older vocabulary. Qwen3-Max and the Meta-built system both named it a straightforward case of direct interior monologue. GPT-5.4 insisted instead on a “hybrid” label, arguing that the vegetal imagery, “my hair is made of leaves” and “my body is a stalk,” was “too central to be reduced to incidental figurative language,” that the boy’s panic was being rendered through the metaphor of becoming a hedge rather than merely decorated by it and thus not a total transcription of the character’s interiority. Here, the passage sits between two classifiable techniques, denying pure identification with one of them. This is a pattern that recurs throughout the corpus: readers converge tightly on where a passage sits on Cohn’s continuous scale far more often than they converge on what discrete name to give that position, which is one reason this chapter treats the scale, not the label, as the more trustworthy measurement. And it is the reasoning traces themselves, not just the numbers, that let us see this happening in specific, quotable language, rather than inferring it from statistics alone.

**Author Fingerprints Across the Corpus**

Cohn's scale is not, on its own, a computational instrument. Applying it to a single passage is an act of close reading; applying it consistently to eleven thousand passages across twenty-one novels requires a method that can hold a stable rubric across an enormous number of individual interpretive judgments without simply averaging away the disagreement that any two careful readers would have about some of them. The figures that follow are drawn from the full corpus: 11,952 passages and 72,167 individual (passage, reader, run) judgments at three repeated runs. 

Before trusting a score of this kind, we needed to know whether it was tracking something real about the prose or simply reproducing whatever a language model’s prior notion of “stream of consciousness” already was. We ran two independent checks. The first tests mediation level against a small set of features computed without reading for meaning at all: the density of first-person pronouns, the ratio of past to non-past tense, and the density of internal punctuation (dashes, ellipses, semicolons). If the scale were merely an artifact of model idiosyncrasy, there is no reason these counts should predict it. They do: passages with a higher proportion of first-person language score reliably lower on the mediation scale, closer to the character's own voice, and passages with a higher proportion of past-tense narration score reliably higher, closer to the narrator's, both relationships strong and significant across a sampled validation set. One prediction made going in—that heavier dash-and-ellipsis punctuation would track *lower* mediation, the visual signature of a mind interrupting itself—ran backward: passages dense with any kind of internal punctuation actually skew toward more mediated, more narratorially controlled prose, consistent with our earlier observation that Woolf’s elaborate, subordinated syntax pulls toward psycho-narration rather than away from it. A specific, correctable prediction failing for an identifiable reason is, if anything, more reassuring than a set of results that had simply confirmed every expectation going in.

The second check answers a sharper worry, one specific to canonical modernism: that a model asked how mediated a *Mrs Dalloway* or *Ulysses* passage is may be retrieving the critical consensus about that novel rather than reading the passage itself. LLMs are demonstrably capable of reproducing well-known public-domain texts closely enough to raise exactly this concern, and *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *The Waves*, *Ulysses*, and Faulkner's two novels are among the most-discussed works in the corpus. We tested this directly by masking character names and other title-identifying features in a stratified sample of 177 passages from six of these titles and rescoring the masked versions in a blind fashion. If the model were substantially retrieving the book's reputation rather than reading it, masking should produce large, inconsistent shifts. It does not: masked scores correlate with original scores at 0.902 across the sample, with a mean shift of \-0.042 on the 0–3 scale and a mean *absolute* shift of only 0.253- noise, not a systematic collapse. The correlation is not uniform across titles (it is highest for *To the Lighthouse*, at 0.95, and lowest for *Mrs Dalloway*, at 0.74, itself a small, specific finding worth further attention rather than an average to be trusted blindly), but nowhere does it suggest the scale is mostly reading the byline. 

Taken together, these two checks do not prove that a mediation score is equivalent to a trained narratologist's judgment; two LLMs sharing similar training data and similar failure modes is not the same thing as two independent human coders, and we do not claim otherwise. But they do show that the number answers to the passage's own grammar and content, not to a model’s untethered impression of what a famous novel ought to sound like.

With the scale validated, the first question is the simplest: does the mediation level, averaged across an author's entire output, differ from author to author in a way that aligns with anything criticism would recognize? It does. Faulkner's two novels average 2.07 on the 0–3 scale, with a spread (standard deviation) of 1.23 and fifty-nine percent of all his scored passages landing at the least mediated extreme, autonomous monologue, the profile of a writer whose SoC prose oscillates between very little mediation and quite a lot of it, rarely settling in the middle. Henry James sits at the opposite pole: a mean of 0.44, a spread of only 0.58, and sixty percent of his passages at the most mediated level, psycho-narration,  a narrator who reports on consciousness from outside and rarely relinquishes that position, which is exactly the role James's fiction is usually assigned in accounts of the novel's prehistory to stream of consciousness proper. Joyce, the writer against whom Woolf's supposed quietness is conventionally measured, averages 1.37, roughly a third of his passages at each of level 0, level 1, and level 3, a genuinely bimodal-leaning profile rather than a settled one. Dorothy Richardson, whose *Pilgrimage* volumes are usually grouped with Woolf as the other major "technique" writer of the group, averages 0.91 with a comparatively tight spread of 0.71, sixty-one percent of her passages clustering specifically at narrated monologue, the most concentrated single-level profile of any author in the corpus.

And Woolf: a mean of 0.82, a spread of 0.88, forty-three percent of her passages at psycho-narration and forty percent at narrated monologue, with only seventeen percent reaching quoted or autonomous monologue at all. On the numbers alone, this places her prose closer to Richardson's and James's than to Faulkner's or, on this measure, even Joyce's, as far as it goes, evidence for exactly the "quiet" reputation the field has assigned her. Joyce's own developmental arc across the corpus's three Joyce titles confirms a version of the standard account on its own terms: *Dubliners* (1914) averages 0.38, *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* (1916) averages 0.69, and *Ulysses* (1922) averages 1.77, a monotonic march toward less mediation that matches the way literary history usually tells Joyce's story, one novel's technique building on the last.

But an author-level mean, however well validated, describes only the center of a distribution, and the word "quiet" has always implied not just where Woolf's prose sits on average but that it sits there consistently, novel after novel, in a way that would make her body of work legible as a single style. That claim is testable independently of the mean, and it is where the numbers stop confirming the standard account.

**Woolf’s SOC Variation, Novel to Novel**

The reception history this chapter has been describing treats Woolf's SoC as, in effect, a fixed style: quieter than Joyce's, more grammatically composed, essentially the same technique deployed across a career. Cohn's scale lets us test that assumption directly, not by asking how Woolf compares to other authors on average, but by asking how much a given Woolf novel's mean mediation score differs from the mean of her other novels, whether “Woolf's SOC” is one thing measured ten times, or ten things that happen to share a name.

It is closer to the second. Using an intraclass correlation (ICC),  a variance-components measure of between-title consistency that corrects for the fact that an author with more titles in a sample is otherwise structurally advantaged relative to an author with fewer,  Woolf's ten novels score 0.561, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of \[0.534, 0.588\]: more than half of the total variation in mediation level across her corpus falls between books rather than within them. No other author in the comparative set comes close on titles that are genuinely independent works. Joyce scores 0.358 \[0.322, 0.391\]; Faulkner, on only two titles, scores 0.037 \[0.006, 0.085\]; Henry James scores 0.008 \[0.000, 0.022\]. Richardson scores 0.001, essentially zero, but this needs an immediate caveat rather than a footnote: *Pilgrimage*'s volumes are installments of one continuous narrative rather than independent novels, so near-zero between-volume variance is close to a definitional fact about serial fiction, not a finding about a fixed authorial style, and we do not use her as a comparison case on this axis for that reason.

It would be easy to assume this entire result is *The Waves* doing all the work, and a large part of it is: leave-one-title-out sensitivity shows that removing *The Waves* alone drops Woolf's ICC from 0.561 to 0.199. That is a real and important qualification. It is not, however, the whole story, for two reasons. First, 0.199 is still higher than Faulkner's or James's full-corpus figures, and higher than Joyce's own score after removing *his* single most extreme title, *Ulysses*, which falls to 0.117 once excluded; Woolf minus her most unusual novel is still more variable, book to book, than Joyce minus his. Second, removing any of Woolf's other nine titles individually moves the ICC only slightly, from 0.563 (without *Flush*) to 0.612 (without *The Years*), meaning that whatever is driving Woolf's between-book variance beyond *The Waves* is distributed across her career rather than concentrated in some second outlier. Nor is the finding an artifact of including *Flush* and *Orlando*, Woolf's two genre-heterogeneous mock-biographies, in a sample otherwise composed of more conventional novels; excluding both actually raises Woolf's ICC to 0.586. However the calculation is sliced, two claims survive: *The Waves* is genuinely, measurably discontinuous with the rest of Woolf's fiction, more discontinuous than any single title's departure from any other author's baseline in this corpus; and the other nine novels, considered on their own, still vary by book more than a "settled style" account predicts.

A mean, though, discards exactly the thing narratologists are usually most interested in about free indirect discourse: not a passage's average distance from the narrator, but its *movement*, the sentence-by-sentence oscillation between voices that gives the technique its characteristic texture. A mean of 0.87 for *Mrs Dalloway* is compatible with prose that alternates violently between levels 0 and 2, or with prose that sits flatly at level 1 throughout,  and criticism has generally assumed the former. The full within-title distributions, and a measure of how strongly one passage's level predicts the next passage's level (a lag-1 autocorrelation along the text), let us ask directly which picture is closer to true, novel by novel.

The answer varies enough to complicate the mean-level story on its own terms. *Mrs Dalloway* (mean 0.89) is in fact the most internally concentrated of Woolf's mid-range novels: seventy-four percent of its passages land at narrated monologue specifically, with only seven percent reaching level 2 and one percent level 3, a narrow, mid-scale band rather than an oscillation between extremes. *To the Lighthouse* (mean 0.76) looks similar: sixty-seven percent at narrated monologue, only four percent above it. *The Years* (mean 0.98), despite an almost identical mean to *Mrs Dalloway*, has a visibly different shape: twenty-seven percent at level 0, forty-nine percent at level 1, and a full twenty-four percent at level 2,  a book that reaches further up the scale far more often than *Dalloway* does, even though the two novels' averages would suggest near-identical technique. *Jacob's Room* (mean 0.59) splits almost evenly between levels 0 and 1 (51% and 39%), a genuinely bimodal-leaning profile that its middling mean conceals entirely. *The Waves* alone, as expected, sits overwhelmingly at the far end: sixty-seven percent of its passages at autonomous monologue, only four percent at psycho-narration, the closest thing in the entire Woolf corpus to Faulkner's profile rather than James's.

Autocorrelation adds a further, and in some ways more surprising, axis of variation, one no account of Woolf's "quiet" style has had the resolution to notice before. *Between the Acts* (0.366), *Orlando* (0.325), and *To the Lighthouse* (0.315) show the strongest tendency for a passage's mediation level to predict the next passage's level,  registers that, once entered, tend to be sustained for a stretch of text, comparable in strength to Faulkner's *As I Lay Dying* (0.327) and well above most of Richardson's *Pilgrimage* volumes. *Flush* (0.050) and *Jacob's Room* (0.051), by contrast, sit near the floor of the entire corpus, indistinguishable in this respect from Henry James's most narratorially stable novel, *The Wings of the Dove* (0.022): register that moves passage to passage almost independently of what came before, regardless of a mean that, in *Jacob's Room*'s case, is itself already the most volatile-by-distribution of any Woolf novel below *The Waves*. *Mrs Dalloway* (0.177) and *The Years* (0.164) land in between, closer to the low end. What this shows is that "how consistently a Woolf novel holds its register" and "how mediated a Woolf novel is on average" are not the same variable and do not move together: *Between the Acts* is both comparatively mediated and comparatively sustained in that mediation, holding a chosen register for a stretch of prose at a time; *Jacob's Room* is comparatively unmediated on average and almost entirely unpredictable passage to passage, its narrator's distance from Jacob's mind resetting with each new paragraph rather than settling into a discernible pattern. Two novels can share a mean and differ completely in texture, or share a texture and differ in mean; Woolf's ten novels do both, in different combinations, novel by novel.

Two further observations belong here, both of which run counter to a career-arc reading of these results. *The Waves* (1931) sits chronologically between *Orlando* (1928) and *Flush* (1933), two of the most heavily mediated novels in Woolf's output; the departure is not the culmination of a steady drift toward less mediation across her career, but a single, bounded event, bracketed on both sides by a return to something closer to her earlier register. And across the full twenty-one-title corpus, a passage-level test for a diachronic trend toward less classifiable, more taxonomy-resistant prose over the 1902–1941 span found no reliable trend at all (Spearman's rho \= 0.104, p \= 0.655): whatever is happening across Woolf's career, it is not part of a broader modernist drift that a mere publication date predicts. If Woolf's fiction has a shape, it is not a line moving toward one destination; it is closer to a set of discrete, revisited choices, of which *The Waves* is the one time she declined the choice she otherwise kept making.

*Close Reading at Scale: "Time Passes"*

The corpus-wide results speak to Woolf's SoC across whole novels; it is worth also asking what the same method can do at the scale of a single, already-famous crux. "Time Passes," the short middle section of *To the Lighthouse* in which the Ramsays' house stands empty for ten years and Mrs Ramsay's death is disposed of in a single bracketed aside, is a canonical site of exactly this kind of narratorial strangeness in Woolf criticism, a section critics have long treated as doing something formally unusual, without always agreeing on what. It is also, in our data, the single site in two full novels' worth of scored passages where a reader's antimimetic flag ,  reserved for a passage that resists placement on Cohn's scale altogether, for an explicit textual reason, not merely low confidence,  actually fires. That made it worth a properly powered second look: rescoring all thirty-three passages of the section at ten runs per reader instead of three, rather than resting an interpretive claim on a single low-probability hit.

"Time Passes" is famous for its strangeness, and the natural assumption going in was that strangeness would register as *less* mediated prose: thought breaking further free of narratorial control, the mind rendered rawer and closer to its own voice, as in Faulkner's or Joyce's more radical passages. It turns out to be the opposite. Across the section, mediation scores skew markedly toward the heavily mediated end of the scale compared to the novel's baseline, and the great majority of its stable, SoC-positive passages land at psycho-narration, the *most* mediated point on Cohn's scale, not the least. Whatever is strange about "Time Passes," then, is not that Woolf's narrator recedes; it is that the narrator's ordinary move, interpreting a mind from outside, "she felt," "it seemed as if," keeps firing on content that increasingly has no mind, in any ordinary sense, to interpret.

That displacement sits in almost no space at all: two sentences, out of thirty-three passages, in which mental predicates are given not to a character but to something that is not a person. In one, "certain airs, detached from the body of the wind," are described entering the house "questioning and wondering," asking, "Were they allies? Were they enemies?" In the other, the room's own silence is personified the same way, "weaving" the cries of birds and the drone of the fields into itself and "folding them round the house in silence," the same rendering of thought-like agency in something that has no mind to think it. On these two sentences, and on almost no others in the whole sample, our two most consistent readers, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max, come apart in a way that is not noise. Across ten repeated readings of the first sentence, GPT-5.4 does not scatter across many answers; it lands, almost evenly, on two distinct positions, each independently reproduced: five readings that hold the passage on the scale, under strain, as an unusually mediated simulation of a state of mind, and five that push it off the scale altogether, arguing that thought-like inquiry without a "plausible single consciousness to own it" cannot be assigned a mediation level built on the assumption that rendered consciousness has a human owner. Qwen3-Max, reading the identical sentence, registers almost no strain at all: nine of ten readings place it confidently at psycho-narration, the personified airs treated as ordinary narratorial imagery rather than as anything the scale cannot hold.

This is, in miniature, close to the theoretical debate Monika Fludernik and, following her, Jan Alber and Brian Richardson have had about "unnatural" narrative technique more broadly: whether devices that seem to exceed realist consciousness-representation are a genuinely distinct category, or whether they are simply conventions not yet naturalized by a given reader's expectations. Our data does not answer that debate, but it does show it actually happening in a specific, quotable pair of sentences, between two readers with different priors about how far a figure of speech can carry an interior state before it stops being a figure and starts being a problem for the scale itself. It also lets us say something more precise than "Woolf's SoC sometimes exceeds measurement": across the whole of two novels, this device,  thought-like predicates attached to a non-human antecedent,  is the one place, nearly the only place, where whether Cohn's scale holds at all depends on which reader, human or machine, is doing the holding.

**Conclusion: Restraint as a Repeated Choice**

This data does not yield a corrected measure of how quiet Woolf's SoC really is relative to Joyce's, nor a definitive answer to whether she belongs among literature's more or less radical stream-of-consciousness writers, and it would be a mistake to read it that way. The comparative frame this chapter opened by describing, Woolf measured against a chaos she declines, a Joycean radicalism she does not attempt, turns out to survive at the level of the author-wide mean: on average, across her whole output, Woolf's prose really is more mediated than Joyce's or Faulkner's, more concentrated at psycho-narration and narrated monologue, closer to Richardson's profile than to theirs. What does not survive is the assumption bundled inside that comparison, that the mean describes a *style*, something Woolf does consistently, novel after novel, the way "chaos" is assumed to be something Joyce does consistently. Nine of her ten novels occupy a band on Cohn's scale that is far wider and far more internally varied, in both level and texture, than a single adjective like "quiet" can hold; the tenth, *The Waves*, leaves that band entirely, more radically unmediated by our measure than either of Faulkner's novels, and does so as a single bounded event in the middle of her career rather than its culmination. The right description of Woolf's stream of consciousness, on this evidence, is not a fixed point on a scale running from control to chaos, but a record of a writer choosing restraint, repeatedly, in different ways in different books, and once, deliberately, choosing not to.

This is also what a computational method can contribute to a problem that close reading alone has spent a century worrying at without resolving. It was never going to settle whether SoC is a genre or a technique, or produce a scale finer than Cohn's that finally holds without remainder. Two large language models trained on similar data and prone to similar failure modes are not independent coders in the sense inter-rater reliability normally requires, and a computational finding, however well validated against surface features and blind to a novel's title, still answers a question a critic posed to it rather than discovering the question on its own. But the method can apply an existing, theoretically grounded scale with total consistency across an entire body of work, at a resolution no reader could sustain unaided across twenty-one novels and eleven thousand passages; it can locate, with precision, where that consistency is real and where it is a mean concealing real variation underneath; and it can follow the one place the scale itself breaks down all the way to two sentences and a disagreement each reader can actually articulate. This chapter's contribution is a way of seeing, at the scale of a career rather than a passage, that what Woolf's readers have been calling one style was several, and that the moment she left all of them behind can be dated, measured, and read closely, on the same page.
