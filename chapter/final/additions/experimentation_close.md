If that shape is not a line, it is worth saying what it is instead, because the
alternative has a name the period's criticism has reserved for other writers.
Set Woolf's sequence beside Joyce's. His three novels, given above, climb
without once reversing: each sits further from the narrator than the one before.
That is development in the ordinary sense, a technique found, extended, and
pushed to its limit, and it is the shape literary history has always given him.
Woolf's ten run 0.39, 0.37, 0.59, 0.89, 0.76, 0.51, 2.50, 0.37, 0.98, 1.02.
Five of her nine transitions move toward less mediation and four move back.
*Flush*, in 1933, sits at 0.37, which is where *Night and Day* sat in 1919, six
novels and eighteen years earlier, and within a hundredth of where she had begun
in *The Voyage Out*. A writer extending a technique could not have written
*Flush* after *The Waves*. A writer for whom the degree of mediation is itself
the variable under test could, and did.

This is where the quiet reading mistook its unit rather than its measurement.
The experimentation the period is known for is sentence-level: Joyce's syntax,
the grammatical disfigurements, prose that advertises its difficulty on the page.
Measured that way Woolf is the quieter writer, and the field's verdict follows
reasonably from its premise. But the scale locates her experiment one level up.
What varies across her career is not the sentence but each book's whole
settlement with the minds it renders: how close the narration will stand, how
much apparatus it will keep, whether consciousness will be reported, inflected,
quoted, or simply let run. Every novel re-opens that question, answers it
differently, and the answers do not accumulate.

Both things are true at once, and the distinction is what the measurement buys:
Woolf's prose is less grammatically disruptive than Joyce's *and* her career is
more internally various than his. The second claim stayed invisible for as long
as experimentation was assumed to be something a sentence does. It becomes
visible the moment one asks of each book in turn how far it stands from the mind
it renders, which is the question Cohn's scale was built to ask, and the question
a corpus is needed to ask ten times over.
