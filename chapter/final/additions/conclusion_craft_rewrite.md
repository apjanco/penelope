**Conclusion: Restraint as a Repeated Choice**

Read along Cohn's scale, novel by novel, Woolf's stream of consciousness does not have a single position, and the familiar description of it as "quiet" turns out to be true only as an average. Taken across her whole output, her prose does sit nearer the narrator's end of the scale than Joyce's or Faulkner's, concentrated at psycho-narration and narrated monologue and closer to Richardson's profile than to theirs; in that sense the comparison that has organized so much Woolf criticism survives. What it carried with it does not: the assumption that the average describes a *style*, something Woolf does the same way from one book to the next. More of the variation in her prose falls between novels than within them, a larger share than for any other writer we examined, and that remains true with *The Waves* set aside. Her other nine novels spread across a band of the scale wider, and more various in texture, than any single adjective can hold: *Mrs Dalloway* concentrated narrowly at narrated monologue, *The Years* reaching quoted monologue far more often from a nearly identical mean, *Jacob's Room* sitting on the seam where narrator and character are hardest to tell apart. *The Waves* leaves that band altogether, and does so as a bounded event in the middle of her career, bracketed by *Orlando* and *Flush*, rather than as the culmination of a drift. What Woolf's readers have called one style was several, and the record those novels leave is of a writer choosing restraint repeatedly, in different ways in different books, and once, deliberately, choosing not to.

That finding matters beyond Woolf, because it changes what kind of object an author's technique is. Criticism has tended to describe a modernist's stream of consciousness by its most characteristic novels, Woolf by *Mrs Dalloway* and *To the Lighthouse*, Joyce by *Ulysses*, and then to set those signature positions against one another as if each marked the author's settled place on the scale. Read at the length of a career, the more telling signature may be how far, and how often, a writer moves. Faulkner's career, read across all eight of his novels in our corpus, has the same shape as Woolf's: an excursion to the unmediated end in *The Sound and the Fury* and *As I Lay Dying*, and then a return. Neither career looks like the progressive radicalization that modernism's premium on the break has taught us to expect, and across the corpus as a whole we found no reliable trend toward less classifiable prose over time. If the most experimental modernist novels are excursions from which their authors came back, rather than destinations they arrived at, then the history of stream of consciousness is less the story of a technique discovered and pushed forward than of a range writers moved within, chose among, and returned from. That is closer to how Cohn described the form than to how it has usually been taught.

Several questions follow that this chapter cannot settle. The first is why Woolf came back. The return after *The Waves* is visible in the measurement but not explained by it; the drafts of *The Waves*, *Flush*, and *The Years*, and the diary entries that track her thinking about each, are where a reader might test whether that return was a considered aesthetic choice, a reaction, or something else again. The second concerns the seam between psycho-narration and narrated monologue, where our readers disagree most often and where *Jacob's Room* sits. Our two principal readers were trained on similar data and are not independent coders in the sense inter-rater reliability requires; a panel of human readers scoring a sample of passages along that boundary would tell us whether the disagreement belongs to the machines or to the prose. The third is scope. Whether excursion and return is a pattern of modernist careers or only of these two would mean reading further careers the same way, and, for Woolf herself, her short fiction, where several of the experiments that led to *Jacob's Room* were first tried. A method that applies Cohn's scale consistently across tens of thousands of passages cannot tell us what a novel means. It can tell us where to look, and on this evidence the place to look is not Woolf's level but her movement.
