**Conclusion: Was Woolf Ever "Quiet"?**

Was Virginia Woolf ever "quiet"? Her innovations in stream of consciousness were no quieter than Joyce's or Faulkner's; they took a different form. Where *Ulysses* and *The Sound and the Fury* announce themselves through broken syntax and unframed monologue, Woolf's experiment lay in modulation: in how she mixed the levels of mediation, and in how differently she mixed them from one novel to the next. No writer we examined modulates as much. More of the variation in her prose falls between her novels than within them, a larger share than for any other writer we examined, and that holds even once *The Waves* is set aside. *Mrs Dalloway* concentrates narrowly at narrated monologue; *The Years*, from a nearly identical average, reaches quoted monologue far more often; *Jacob's Room* sits on the seam where narrator and character are hardest to tell apart; *Between the Acts* and *To the Lighthouse* hold a register for long stretches, while *Flush* and *Jacob's Room* reset it from one passage to the next; and *The Waves* leaves the narrator behind almost entirely, then is followed by *Flush*, among her most mediated books. None of this is visible in a single passage, however carefully read. It becomes visible only when Woolf's whole oeuvre is read along one scale, consistently, against a corpus of her peers. Read that way, what earlier criticism heard as quietness is the sound of a writer changing her instrument from book to book.

Reading Woolf this way also changes the shape of the history she is usually placed in. Robert Humphrey's *Stream of Consciousness in the Modern Novel* (1954), subtitled *A Study of James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, Dorothy Richardson, William Faulkner, and Others*, defined the form by a roster of authors, and criticism has largely kept that roster as its map: stream of consciousness is what these writers did. Read across their careers, however, stream of consciousness in its strictest sense, thought set on the page with no narrator in between, belongs less to the writers than to a handful of their books. *Ulysses*, *The Sound and the Fury*, *As I Lay Dying*, and *The Waves* together account for nearly three-quarters of all the passages our readers placed at autonomous monologue, and all four were published within a single decade, between 1922 and 1931. The typical novel in the corpus, by any of these writers, contains almost none. Nor do these four books stand at the end of a progression. Faulkner follows *As I Lay Dying* with *Sanctuary*, and returns to the more mediated register of his earlier fiction; Woolf follows *The Waves* with *Flush*; Joyce's corpus ends with *Ulysses*, and his earlier fiction is, if anything, more mediated than Woolf's average. The "chaos" against which Woolf's control has so often been measured is, on this evidence, largely the property of a single book, and to set Woolf's whole career against *Ulysses* is to set a range against a single point. The range is where Woolf's experiment lies.

What does change gradually is something quieter in the literal sense. Across the corpus, and within the careers of six of our seven writers, psycho-narration gives way, novel by novel, to narrated monologue, the character's thought carried in the narrator's grammar. Woolf moves this way, from *The Voyage Out* to *Between the Acts*; so does James across his career; so, most strikingly, does May Sinclair, whose novels shift toward narrated monologue in the very years in which she was giving the stream of consciousness its name. Only Richardson, whose *Pilgrimage* holds a single register across twelve volumes, does not. This is a trend that earlier computational histories of the "inward turn" were not built to see. Studies that count the verbs by which narrators report a mind, across French fiction and across ten European literatures, have found no rise in inwardness at all; but narrated monologue is precisely the mode that dispenses with "she thought" and "he felt," so a shift toward it could leave such counts flat even as fiction moves closer to its characters' minds. Taken together, these results suggest a history of modernist interiority that is neither the rupture modernism claimed for itself nor the absence of change that verb counts report. The inward turn was real, but it was gradual and widely shared, and it ran toward the narrator's borrowed voice rather than away from it, while the radical end of the scale was reached only in a few bounded excursions, from which their authors came back or after which they wrote no more. Woolf's place in that history is not at either extreme. It is in the movement between them, and in the fact that she made that movement, in one direction and then another, more often and more deliberately than anyone else we read.

Several questions follow that mediation alone cannot answer. The first concerns whose consciousness is being rendered. Cohn's scale assumes a mind that belongs to a character, and the places where our readers strained against their categories were places where that assumption gave way: the two sentences of "Time Passes" in which thought-like predicates attach to the airs and the silence of an empty house, and which both readers nonetheless placed at psycho-narration, rendered from outside. Woolf's fiction offers several neighbouring cases in which the owner of a consciousness is uncertain, collective, or not human: the six voices of *The Waves*, which speak in one idiom and are sometimes read as facets of a single mind; the village audience of *Between the Acts*, whose fragments of overheard thought belong to no one in particular; and the dog whose mind *Flush* renders almost entirely from outside. Beyond Woolf, the corpus contains retrospective first-person narrators, such as Dowell in *The Good Soldier*, whom our readers sometimes placed near the unmediated end of the scale, and the shifting monologists of *As I Lay Dying*, each of whom speaks without a narrator but not without an audience. A second dimension of measurement, asking not only how far the narrator stands from a mind but whether there is a single, human mind to stand away from, would allow these cases to be compared directly rather than treated as exceptions.

A second set of questions concerns texture. A mean level, and even a full distribution, describes a novel as if its passages could be shuffled without loss; the autocorrelation measure used here is a first, coarse attempt to capture how a register actually moves through a book, and it already separates novels whose averages are nearly identical. Finer measures, of oscillation within a single passage, of how long a register is held before it shifts, and of how mediation interacts with narrative pace and with the handling of time, could test directly the critical intuition that free indirect discourse is defined by its movement rather than by its position. The same methods could be extended beyond this corpus: to Woolf's short fiction, where several of the experiments that led to *Jacob's Room* were first tried; to the modernist writers this chapter has not read; and to the popular fiction into which, as other computational work has suggested, stream-of-consciousness technique later spread. Finally, there is the question of the readers themselves. Our two principal readers were trained on similar data and are not independent in the sense inter-rater reliability requires, and the seam between psycho-narration and narrated monologue, where they disagree most often, is exactly where human readers of Woolf have long disagreed too. A panel of human readers scoring the same passages would tell us how much of that disagreement belongs to the machines and how much to the prose. A method that applies Cohn's scale across tens of thousands of passages cannot tell us what a novel means. It can tell us where to look, and on this evidence the place to look in Woolf is not her level but her movement.
