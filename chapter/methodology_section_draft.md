*Methodology*

\[Draft, October 2026. Replaces the "Methodology" stub and the reader paragraph in the working draft. Bracketed notes are for us, not for the chapter.\]

To read a corpus along Cohn's scale, we needed many readers, and readers whose disagreements we could compare. A single critic applying Cohn's categories to fifty novels would give us one sensibility's consistency, with no way to tell where the scale holds and where it gives way. A single computational classifier would be worse: it would give a confident label to every passage, including the ones where confidence is unwarranted. We set out instead to build a panel of independent readers. Each reads every passage more than once and gives reasons for each judgment. Throughout, we treat how often the panel agrees, with itself and with each other, as part of the result rather than as error to be averaged away. This section describes the texts, the readers, what we asked them to do, and the checks we ran before trusting what they told us.

**The corpus**

Our corpus centers on Woolf's ten novels: *The Voyage Out*, *Night and Day*, *Jacob's Room*, *Mrs Dalloway*, *To the Lighthouse*, *Orlando*, *The Waves*, *Flush*, *The Years*, and *Between the Acts*. Around them we placed six other authors, chosen so that Woolf's position could be compared with the writers she is most often measured against, and so that a change in her prose across a career would have other careers to be measured against. Joyce is the comparison the reception history insists on. Dorothy Richardson is the novelist whose work first drew the phrase "stream of consciousness" into literary criticism. May Sinclair is the critic who put it there, in her 1918 review of *Pilgrimage*, and who then wrote in the mode herself; her career, from *Audrey Craven* (1897) to *Life and Death of Harriett Frean* (1922), is the only one in the corpus that crosses the coinage of the term this chapter is about. Faulkner represents the later, American extension of the form. Henry James and Ford Madox Ford serve as a comparison set: novelists deeply concerned with rendering consciousness who are not usually counted among stream-of-consciousness writers, against whom we could see what the scale registers in prose that represents minds without being canonically SOC.

The corpus has deliberate asymmetries, and two of them shape what it can show. First, *Pilgrimage* is not nine novels. Richardson insisted it was a single work in chapters, published serially, so "variation from book to book" means something structurally different for her than for Woolf, whose novels are discrete projects. We report Richardson's volumes separately because that is how they were published and read, but we do not treat their consistency as a choice repeated across independent books. Second, Joyce's career in the corpus ends in 1922, because *Finnegans Wake* remains in copyright in the United States. His arc is therefore truncated at precisely the point where it would become most interesting, and any comparison of career shapes has to say so. Faulkner's public-domain work is similarly limited, to three novels in four years.

To extend those two careers, we supplemented the main corpus with eight further works: five Faulkner novels from *Mosquitoes* (1927) to *Absalom, Absalom!* (1936), and the last three volumes of *Pilgrimage* (1931–1938), 4,296 passages in all. These were read by only one member of the panel. Because no other reader can be checked against it on these texts, we use them only where they bear on a specific claim about Faulkner's or Richardson's careers, and we flag them each time. Several works in both the main and the supplementary corpus remain in copyright in the United States; we describe how they were handled in a note.[^5]

Texts came from Project Gutenberg and Standard Ebooks where available, and otherwise from our own digitized copies.[^1] We divided each novel into passages of roughly 80 to 200 words, following paragraph boundaries wherever possible so that a passage begins and ends where the author's own prose does. A passage of this length is long enough to show a shift in narrative mode (a sentence that begins in the narrator's voice and ends in a character's) and short enough that a single passage seldom contains more than one or two such shifts.

**The readers**

Two large language models did most of the reading: GPT-5.4, OpenAI's flagship general-purpose model at the time of the study, and Qwen3-Max, the equivalent flagship system from Alibaba. Both are "reasoning" models: before answering, each produces a written chain of argument, and we asked each to state its case for the judgment it reached and its case against the strongest alternative. We chose two systems from different companies, trained on different data under different design priorities, on purpose. If both independently place a passage at the same point on Cohn's scale, that agreement is harder to dismiss as one model's idiosyncrasy than if we had asked the same system twice.

A third reader works differently, and that difference is why we included it. Jev, a model built by the company TypeSafe, does not write an argument and then commit to an answer. It is asked each question separately and returns a probability for every possible answer: for example, a 55 percent chance that a passage is narrated monologue, 40 percent psycho-narration, and 5 percent something else. Where the other two readers give us ambiguity only indirectly, through the fact that they disagree, Jev gives a direct measure of how evenly a passage sits between two readings. Its rankings of the works also agree closely with those of the two main readers (rank correlations of 0.94 with GPT-5.4 and 0.87 with Qwen3-Max), which is what licenses using it alone on the eight supplementary works.

Two smaller details complete the panel. Qwen3-Max never read nine passages, scattered across *Ulysses*, *The Sound and the Fury*, *The Ambassadors*, *What Maisie Knew*, and *The Last Post*, because Alibaba's automatic content filters refused to process them. Rather than quietly drop those passages, we had them read instead by an open-weight Qwen model that we ran on university computing resources, three times each, as with the main readers. It is recorded as a separate reader, so that no passage went missing because of a commercial filter's judgment about its content. We also tested a fourth reader, Meta's Llama 3.3, and excluded it. Before admitting any model to the panel, we ran it on a fixed calibration set of 277 passages and checked whether it was making distinctions at all. Llama placed more than ninety percent of its judgments on a single level of the scale and produced fluent, plausible arguments for every one of them. That failure is worth recording, because it is the failure a reader of the outputs alone would be least likely to catch: the arguments read well, but the judgments barely varied.

**What the readers were asked**

Each reader received a passage and a set of instructions setting out Cohn's scale in four levels:

- **Level 0, psycho-narration:** the narrator reports a mind from outside ("She felt uneasy").
- **Level 1, narrated monologue:** the character's thought bends the narrator's sentence without quotation ("Wasn't it odd that he never wrote?").
- **Level 2, quoted monologue:** interior speech is explicitly framed ("I am uneasy, she thought").
- **Level 3, autonomous monologue:** thought is presented with no narratorial frame at all, as in Molly Bloom's soliloquy.

The numbering runs from most mediated to least, so a higher number means the character's consciousness reaches the page with less of the narrator in between. \[Decide whether to keep this direction or flip it; whichever we choose, every later section must use it consistently.\] The instructions told readers that a passage falling between two levels should be placed at the level it leans toward. They also said that two readers one level apart are in near-agreement, while two readers at opposite ends of the scale disagree about the basic nature of the passage.

The readers did more than place each passage on the scale. They first decided whether the passage renders consciousness at all. Then they assigned it one of Humphrey's named techniques, judged whether their two answers fit the passage cleanly or only under strain, and wrote out both their argument and the strongest alternative they had rejected. The Humphrey labels and the "fit" judgment are what our earlier, typology-based analysis rested on (described in the previous section). Here they serve mainly as a check on the scale, and we return to them below.

**Reading more than once**

Each of the two main readers read every passage three times. These models do not give the same answer every time they are asked; run again on the same passage, they can reach a different judgment. We treat that variation as information. A reader that places a passage at the same level on every run has a stable reading of it; a reader that moves between two levels from one run to the next is registering that the passage could go either way. This lets us distinguish a reader that is uncertain about a passage from one that is simply wrong once, and to ask, as earlier scholarship could not, whether a reader disagrees with itself. For the closest readings in this chapter, the thirty-three passages of "Time Passes" discussed below, we raised the number of readings to ten per reader so that a split between two positions could be distinguished from noise. Jev, which reports probabilities directly, reads each passage once.

In total, the main corpus received roughly 76,000 judgments from each of the two main readers and 25,000 from Jev.

**Checks on the instrument**

A scale applied by language models is only useful if it responds to the prose rather than to the models' prior expectations about what "stream of consciousness" should look like. We ran four checks before building any argument on it, and we report one result that limits what the scale can claim.

*Does the scale track the text's grammar?* We compared the scale against features that a simple script can count without reading for meaning, across all 16,953 passages that both main readers identified as rendering consciousness. Passages with a higher proportion of first-person pronouns sit reliably higher on the scale, closer to unframed monologue. Passages with more past-tense verbs sit reliably lower, closer to the narrator's report. Both relationships hold for each reader separately, including Jev. More importantly, they also hold *within* each author's prose, not only between authors. That matters because Faulkner, say, is both more first-person and higher on the scale than James, and a pooled correlation could be carried by that contrast alone.[^2] Dashes and ellipses, the visual signature of a mind interrupting itself, mark less mediated prose as well, though more weakly. Semicolons are a useful caution. Across the corpus as a whole, semicolon-heavy passages sit lower on the scale, but that is a difference between authors, chiefly the long subordinated periods of Henry James. Within any single author's prose, the relationship is negligible and runs, if anything, the other way. A feature that seems to predict mediation can turn out to be predicting the author, which is one more reason we never pool across authors without saying so.

*Is the reader recognizing the book rather than reading the passage?* Language models have read these novels, likely many times, and a model that knows a passage comes from *The Waves* might score it as *The Waves* "should" be scored. To test this, we took a sample of passages from six of the most famous novels in the corpus and removed every proper name and other identifying detail. The readers then scored the masked versions. Scores for masked and original passages correlate at 0.90, with no systematic shift in either direction. The readers are responding substantially to a passage's grammar, not to its title.

*Do the readers agree with each other?* On the passages both main readers identified as rendering consciousness, they place a passage at the same level of Cohn's scale about two times in three. Correcting for chance agreement, this is moderate agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.504). On the same passages, they choose the same one of Humphrey's named techniques only a little over half the time (κ = 0.385), which counts as fair agreement.[^3] More telling than either figure is the shape of the disagreement on the scale. When the readers place a passage differently, nearly nine times in ten they are a single level apart, and across all passages only about one in twenty-four finds them two or more levels apart. They rarely disagree about the basic nature of a passage. They disagree about where a passage sits along a continuum both of them recognize.

Re-expressing the same judgments on the scale also shows where the typology was breaking down. When the readers disagree about Humphrey's techniques, more than four times in ten the disagreement is between indirect interior monologue and omniscient description. No other pair of techniques comes close: the next most frequent accounts for barely one disagreement in sixteen. That is, it is the boundary between Cohn's level 1 and level 0, narrated monologue and psycho-narration. Woolf is most closely associated with the first of these, and her narrator is most often said to fall back on the second. The typology's weakest seam lies exactly where the critical debate about Woolf has always been.

*Are the scale and the typology two measurements or one?* This check limits the claim. For GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-Max, the level a reader assigns is almost entirely determined by the technique it chose. A passage labeled indirect interior monologue is placed at level 1 more than 99 percent of the time, and one labeled omniscient description at level 0 essentially always. Asked to judge both at once, these readers appear to choose a technique and then assign the level that goes with it. Jev, which is asked the two questions separately, does not behave this way. Of the passages Jev identifies as indirect interior monologue, it places only about half at level 1 and some 41 percent at level 0. When the scale is not anchored to a typological choice, the boundary between narrated monologue and psycho-narration becomes visibly porous. For our argument, this means two things. Disagreement between the two main readers about level is partly disagreement about technique carried over. And Jev's probabilities are our best evidence of where along that boundary a passage actually sits.

*Does the quality of the digital text matter?* Every text in the main corpus is a proofread e-text. Some works we would have liked to include, such as Faulkner's *Mosquitoes* and *Sartoris*, exist publicly only as uncorrected scans. Cohn's scale is unusually exposed to scanning errors, because what separates quoted monologue from narrated monologue is often nothing more than a pair of quotation marks, and those are what optical character recognition most often mangles. *Soldiers' Pay* gave us a clean test. The proofread edition in our corpus was itself made from a particular Internet Archive scan, so we could set the same words side by side in both forms. We took 260 passages from the proofread text, found the corresponding stretch of the raw scan, with its running heads, broken hyphenations and lost apostrophes intact, and had both main readers score the scanned versions exactly as they had scored the clean ones. The scanned passages had lost about six percent of their quotation marks. Contrary to our prediction, the lost quotation marks did not push framed interior speech down the scale. GPT-5.4 read the scan essentially as it read the clean text: its agreement with its own clean readings fell no further than the ordinary variation between its repeated readings. Qwen3-Max, however, was measurably less steady on the scan. It more often concluded that a passage rendered no consciousness at all, and where it did score the passage, it matched its clean reading markedly less often than it matched itself.[^4] The damage, in other words, is not to the quotation frame specifically but to one reader's confidence in the text generally. Because the effect differs by reader, scanned texts would distort exactly the comparisons between readers on which this chapter depends. We therefore keep them out of the corpus.

**How we report results**

Two principles follow from these checks, and they govern every figure in the rest of the chapter.

First, we report each reader separately and do not pool them. The readers agree on how passages and works are ranked, but not on where the scale's zero point sits. On the same passages, GPT-5.4 places about half of its judgments at level 0 and Qwen3-Max fewer than a third. An average across the two would mix two calibrations of the instrument rather than measure the text. A claim that holds under one reader and not another is reported as such. A claim we make about Woolf has to survive all three readers.

Second, when we summarize a novel, we look at the full distribution of its passages across the four levels, not just their mean. A novel averaging 0.9 might sit evenly at level 1 throughout, or alternate between 0 and 2. These are different kinds of writing, and the difference between them is often what a comparison turns on.
