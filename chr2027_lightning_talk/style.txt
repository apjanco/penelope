Introduction
“Stream of consciousness” (SoC) narration is a ubiquitous term in modernist studies and fiction generally, yet it has been notoriously difficult to characterize or categorize. As a broad literary genre, SoC has come to delineate works that “attempt to describe in great detail the experience of reality as lived by an idiosyncratic individual” by showing “not necessarily how things really are, but how things are experienced” (Lewis 158). Beyond this essential definition, though, literary critics have diverged on SoC’s particularities, agreeing that it is a measurable element of style but not yet agreeing on what those measurable features and categories are. In their large-scale computational study of SoC narration in fiction, Hoyt Long and Richard Jean So characterize the literary technique as inherently ambiguous: “no one can agree on what [SoC] is, yet everyone seems to know it when they see it” (346). For such a widely-discussed literary phenomenon, it is striking that it continues to be undecidable in its particularities—and also that it endures as something which seems to want to be particularized. 
The long-standing critical desire to define the mechanisms and features of SoC suggests that it is exactly its porous boundaries which makes it so available for analysis. As Rebecca Bowler and Claire Drewery summarize in the introduction to a special issue on SOC, “a definitive definition of the elusive label ‘stream of consciousness’ is as fraught with contradictions and complexities as it was a century earlier when the term was first conceptualized” (2) [can give a longer footnote about the contention around this term if necessary; cite Richardson’s criticism of the term]. Decades of narratological scholarship—from Robert Humphrey’s foundational 1954 taxonomy onward [note to self to check if there’s anything significant earlier]—have proposed competing schemes for classifying SoC’s techniques (interior monologue, free indirect discourse, free association, orthographic markers, and more). Yet thus far there is no critical consensus on such techniques, nor a clear understanding of where and how techniques blur, where one shades into another. 
We argue that this persistent disagreement is not a defect to be engineered away, but the most useful signal a computational method can capture about the form. We are not interested in creating a definitive typology of SoC (we can’t) nor in verifying whether the typologies that others have created are definitive (they aren’t). Instead, we are interested in the affordances that applying one systematic typology can grant towards understanding a particular author’s nuanced use of SoC in a novel. If we had to explain to ourselves, concretely, what categories and features of SOC there are—and where the blurriness between them lies—could we? What happens to our understanding of SOC within a text when we attempt to categorize pieces of that text with these distinctions? We create and test a pipeline that fine-tunes a local language model (Qwen3-4B) to detect and classify SoC passages in literary prose against a ten-class taxonomy synthesizing Humphrey and Erwin Steinberg’s The Stream of Consciousness and Beyond in Ulysses (1973). Our corpus centers on Virginia Woolf’s Mrs Dalloway and To the Lighthouse, alongside a comparative set spanning Dorothy Richardson, James Joyce, William Faulkner, and May Sinclair. 
Woolf presents a useful test case for our questions because of her own “ambiguous” relationship to SoC, as both a genre and a set of techniques. Woolf is frequently cited as one of the foremost pioneers of SoC narration, but, as Dora Zhang has counterintuitively noted, “despite the ubiquitous familiarity of [SoC] in general, and its association with Woolf in particular, her innovations in this arena have not been clearly recognized” (133). Woolf is thus highly legible for understanding the categories of SoC generally, and she also is perhaps in need of further analysis which acknowledges the complex undecidability of her SoC technique. Indeed, crafting classificatory and interpretive ambiguity is one of Woolf’s primary aims—to create fiction which exposes “the limits of what we can know of other people” as well as the way “we read each other according to such predetermined categories” (Zhang 141). This for Woolf is always a writerly problem as much as it is a social or representational one, and her SoC intricately encodes the “limits” of determinacy in its very movements between narrative modes.

Woolf’s Ambiguous Stream of Consciousness
Woolf’s long-standing association with the rise of SoC seems to come at odds with the idea that her particular contribution to the form is undertheorized. We chalk this discrepancy up to three key points: one, that Woolf’s relative adherence to grammatical sentence structure makes her writing seem more constructed rather than organic, which again pushes against conceptions of SoC as something that renders thought “naturally” on the page; two, that Woolf as a SoC innovator traditionally has come second to Joyce, which has perpetuated the narrative that she is slightly less innovative when compared to the standards set by Joyce’s own SoC techniques; and three, that her predominant use of free indirect discourse or, to use Humphrey’s term, “indirect interior monologue,” leads to the assumption that her narrative technique is more conventional. These assumptions, as they appear in scholarship, are interrelated; they all boil down to the idea that there is a standard—and more innovative—narrative about the development of SoC as a technique that Woolf does not quite adhere to. We suggest, though, that the acknowledged technical ambiguity of Woolf’s movement between SoC modes is itself part of her unique contribution to the form and its opening of narrative possibilities.
Originating from William James’ description of human thought as “flow” rather than “jointed”-ness, the metaphor of the “stream” of consciousness has often been understood to be a representation of individual reality as an unmediated continuity between moments, observations, thoughts, and ideas. Novelist May Sinclair first imported the term into literature when reviewing the first three novel-chapters of Dorothy Richardson’s Pilgrimage, a 13-volume novel sequence in which Richardson narrates the inner life of a single female protagonist, Miriam Henderson. With the term, Sinclair also imported its metaphorical assumptions, writing that Richardson’s narration completely overlaps with Miriam’s life and thoughts: “moments of Miriam’s consciousness pass one by one” in Jamesian flow, bringing us “closer to reality than any” (Sinclair 58). The modernist project of representing a different reality of perception—which manifests differently with each writer—has become subsumed under this relatively monolithic metaphor for that project. Though scholars and practitioners of SoC themselves have complicated and taken issue with this understanding of SoC, its origins in psychology in a way have predetermined its association with both unformedness and single-mindedness. Such an understanding puts Woolf in the back seat, for “while [SoC] is famously associated with irregular or absent punctuation, fragmented or incomplete sentences, ellipses, and discontinuous syntax, as in Molly Bloom’s soliloquy at the end of Ulysses, Woolf’s innovations in stream of consciousness writing announce themselves more quietly” (Zhang 133). The idea that Woolf’s “innovations” in SoC are “quieter” than the others’ points to the impression that Woolf’s writing is more grammatical and constructed than loose or organic. Many other critics have either implicitly or explicitly acknowledged Joyce as the foremost innovator of SoC, and often compare Woolf’s own technique to his on the grounds of irregularity, unformedness, and chaos. Such characterizations, while perhaps accurate in their descriptions, suggest a hierarchy of experimentalism in which Joyce’s narration is seen as more radical than everyone else’s. An early monograph on SoC by Shiv K. Kumar proposes Joyce’s writing to be the “perfection of the stream of consciousness” (qtd. Bowler and Drewery 4). In a reading of one passage from [Ulysses?], Anne Fernihough writes that “Joyce’s technique … is more naturalistic than Woolf’s, in that it attempts to render as closely as possible the chaos of the inner life, and the raw, unfinished quality of our thoughts and impressions, as well as the sheer speed with which they meld into one another” (Fernihough 66, emphasis in original). David Dowling notes Woolf’s relative “control” over even the passages which are the most wandering: “Even in the monologues of Septimus, Woolf retains control of point of view and imagery; David Daiches says she had a ‘tidy mind’ compared with that of Joyce” (48). These comparisons emphasize the idea that SoC should in its most technically pure form prioritize the organic “stream” quality embedded in James’ metaphor, what Fernihough describes as “an undiscriminating and unstoppable consciousness in which there is no editing out and no hierarchy of thought” (67). And though many critics have noted that this metaphor has been a contentious one since the beginning (Richardson herself took issue with it), that has not come hand-in-hand with decoupling Joyce’s primary association with the form [might want a footnote on Mieszkowski more on this issue of the metaphor]. That association still results in Joyce overshadowing other SoC innovators, Woolf especially.
The sense that Joyce was more experimental than Woolf comes, perhaps, from each of their relationships to traditional narrative modes. Despite the fact that Joyce also employs a plethora of narrative techniques, Woolf’s particular SoC mode has been seen as more in line with free indirect discourse—a very canonical technique and thus less clearly new. In her study of the representation of consciousness in fiction, Transparent Minds, Dorrit Cohn highly associates Woolf with the technique she calls “narrated monologue,” defined as “a character’s mental discourse in the guise of the narrator’s discourse” and a very close analogue to free indirect discourse (14). This technique, she writes, “needed no Joycean revolution to make it a workable instrument for recording the minutiae of the inner life. Hence it acted as a kind of stylistic bridge that led from the nineteenth- to twentieth-century fiction” (115). In other words, Woolf’s primary choice of SoC technique was less of a clean “break” from tradition and more of a subtle shift, still reflected in style but less so than the “rival technique of the unsignaled ‘Joycean’ monologue” (112). Dora Zhang’s chapter convincingly argues that Woolf revolutionizes free indirect discourse more than others have acknowledged previously, but we suggest that it is still Woolf’s clear association with this technique in particular that puts closer analysis of her SoC on pause. Free indirect discourse is widely studied and attributed to many authors, which may make it seem less imperative to continually investigate Woolf’s version of it. 

Reframing Woolf’s indirect SoC technique as one which prioritizes interpretive ambiguity elucidates her unique contributions to this developing narrative form. Woolf is already widely credited for crafting a SoC which moves fluidly between character perspectives, stemming from her repurposing of free indirect discourse as a tool for representing multiple consciousnesses at once (Zhang 134, Dowling 46-47). Yet perhaps less discussed than this perspectival ambiguity is Woolf’s technical ambiguity, her ability to blur the line between modes of representation itself. In a close reading of a passage from To the Lighthouse, Cohn notes the impossibility of parsing what exact SoC technique Woolf is using: she concludes that the sentence “remains ambiguously suspended between narration and narrated monologue” (133).
